Across the AI supply chain, millions of accelerators are in motion: ordered, manufactured, staged, shipped, queued for facilities that do not yet have power.

At any moment, many of them sit on pallets in warehouses around the world. Climate-controlled, security-gated, quiet. Waiting for a transformer. Waiting for a substation. Waiting for steel from another state.

Some may never be plugged into the slots they were ordered for. For others, one, two, even three generations of silicon will ship before they see a power connection.

The first AI capital cycle is overbuilding the machinery of intelligence before the economy knows how to use it. The chips, asleep on pallets, will go to work anyway. They will reach people who could never have paid to build them.

<span id="i-the-plug"></span>

## I. Waiting for the Grid

The first bottleneck is a place to draw power.

The hyperscalers announced over \$700 billion of AI and cloud infrastructure spending for 2026 alone.<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> Before that spending can deliver intelligence, it must deliver steel, copper, silicon, and concrete to a site with permission to draw power. Amazon told investors European grid connections can take up to seven years. A datacentre takes about two years to build.<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>

<Figure
  src={caPowerLines}
  alt="High-voltage transmission towers and power lines crossing open country under a clear sky"
  caption="The model trains in months. The transformer arrives in years."
  wide={true}
/>

A bridge delayed by two years is still a bridge. A GPU delayed by two years has crossed an architectural boundary. Hopper to Blackwell. Blackwell to Rubin. The scarce asset is the **slot**: the power, cooling, land, equipment, and permission that let a chip go to work. Every order book is a claim on a future slot.

<PullQuote>

Can operators plug chips in before they depreciate?

</PullQuote>

The money is arriving faster than the sites. Record borrowing and venture funding are financing the buildout (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-a">Appendix A</a>). Where a chip finally lands also determines what it costs to run: the same fuel can cost six times as much across an ocean (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-b">Appendix B</a>).

<span id="ii-the-order-book-is-not-demand"></span>

## II. Buying Tomorrow Too Soon

Across the largest named projects, roughly a fifth of the accelerator facility load ordered or planned is actually energised (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-c">Appendix C</a>). In a separate survey of enterprise Kubernetes clusters, Cast AI measured average sustained GPU utilisation of 5% (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-d">Appendix D</a>).<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup> First the chips need power. Then they need work.

Part of the order book buys the right to compute later. In a bottlenecked world, that can be sensible insurance. But the chip ages while its buyer waits. The spending assumes that businesses will find work for it before the frontier moves on.

Autonomous driving shows how long that can take. Google and Waymo demonstrated Level 4 around 2016, impressive enough to convince many observers broad deployment was a year or two away.<sup><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup> A decade later, commercial deployment remains confined to a handful of geofenced cities. Between the demonstration and the service lie years of testing, fleet operations, and public trust. Agentic workflows will follow the same arc.

Coding produced some of AI's clearest early revenue because it entered a field already equipped to test its work. Other industries are still building those checks. The <a class="xref" href="#appendix-m">early revenue and adoption figures</a> show how unevenly capability becomes a business.

<PullQuote>

The order book assumes every industry can adopt AI as quickly as programmers did.

</PullQuote>

Railroads in the 1870s. Telegraph in the 1840s. Fibre in 1999. Again and again, investors see the technology clearly and misjudge the time it will take to use it.

## III. The Depreciation Clock

Premium power goes to premium silicon.

A hyperscaler with a scarce power permit and an even scarcer substation gives that slot to the chip that does the most valuable work per watt. The next architecture arrives, the software follows it, and the rack built for one generation houses its successor. Some of the world's most ambitious computing hardware becomes second-hand before it has been switched on.

And yet the chip does not stop being useful when it stops being frontier. A Hopper-class accelerator from 2023 still performs trillions of multiplications per second. Inference, simulation, and scientific computing can use those operations for years. At a fraction of its original price, it can let a small laboratory explore ideas that would otherwise stay in a notebook.

This is the **plateau**: hardware that has left the frontier and still has a working life ahead of it. While the boom bids up the memory and packaging needed for new chips, older ones lose their premium. Better software keeps finding more for them to do (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-h">Appendix H</a>).

<PullQuote>

The frontier gets more expensive faster than it gets universally better. The plateau gets cheaper faster than it gets obsolete.

</PullQuote>

<span id="iv-the-backbone-and-the-last-mile"></span>

## IV. A Backbone Waiting for Its Last Mile

Between 1996 and 2001, telecom carriers laid millions of miles of long-haul fibre across the United States. WorldCom, Global Crossing, Qwest. The companies collapsed, and much of the fibre lay dark. Buyers inherited the cables for a fraction of what they had cost to lay.

And then it became the spine of the internet.

But it took twenty years. The fibre could carry the traffic long before most households could reach it. Dial-up gave way to broadband; copper gave way to fibre. Netflix and YouTube were waiting on that <a class="xref" href="#appendix-k">last mile</a>.

AI is building the same shape. The compute backbone stretches from Texas to the Gulf and, [when the land runs out, the sea](/essays/leviathan). The models are capable. Getting them into everyday work takes another kind of engineering.

The last mile is trust made practical: a way to check the answer, see what happened, limit what the system may touch, and restore an earlier version when something goes wrong.

Coding got there first because it already had compilers, linters, tests, and version control. They catch many errors in seconds and give each revision something to answer to. Other domains must build comparable checks around their own failures, regulations, and institutions.

The reset may be quieter than the fibre crash. Hyperscalers can move older chips to other jobs inside the same company. Sovereign and enterprise buyers can hold them for years. As the timetable stretches, capacity loses its premium and reaches people who could never have paid the original price.

<PullQuote>

Bubbles build infrastructure.

</PullQuote>

<span id="v-the-repricing-mechanism"></span>

## V. From the Frontier to Ordinary Hands

Follow a chip after it leaves frontier training. Inside a hyperscaler, it moves to inference, then ad ranking, then batch work. The silicon stays in the building while the cost of using it falls. Across company boundaries, the same migration becomes visible: in May 2026, Anthropic took over Colossus 1 while xAI moved frontier training to its newer facility (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-i">Appendix I</a>).<sup><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup>

<Figure
  src={caH100}
  alt="An NVIDIA HGX H100 board, eight gold-topped GPUs on a black baseboard, on a dark reflective surface"
  caption="A Hopper-class accelerator. Hardware displaced from frontier training can still serve inference, simulation, and fine-tuning. Photo: NVIDIA."
  wide={true}
/>

Beyond the hyperscaler, regional clouds, universities, and industrial sites absorb another wave. Beyond them is a workstation in a research lab, a server in a small business, a machine in someone's home. The chip travels down in price and out into the world.

It finds power on a different scale. Frontier training needs hundreds of megawatts behind one substation. Smaller jobs can use spare capacity scattered through offices, laboratories, and existing datacentres. A new accelerator is too expensive to leave idle in a university rack. A written-down one can earn its keep there (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-g">Appendix G</a>). Even today, renting the same chip can cost up to ten times as much through a hyperscaler as through a specialist cloud (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-j">Appendix J</a>).

Older generations already serve years beyond their frontier lives. The bust scales a migration that is already happening. Sometimes the chip changes hands. Sometimes only the number on the customer's bill changes. Either way, someone gains access.

Liquidation makes headlines. Redistribution changes the world.

## VI. Stockfish for Everything

The maximalist version of the AI story imagines one enormous model doing everything. A country of geniuses in a datacentre. A digital workforce replacing labour wholesale.

Stockfish combines a neural evaluator with search. The network judges a position. The search tests what might follow. Move ordering, pruning, endgame tablebases, and statistical testing turn those parts into a formidable chess engine that runs on ordinary CPUs.<sup><a href="#ref-stockfish">[S]</a></sup> Learned judgement becomes reliable inside a system that constrains, tests, and corrects it.

Most economically useful AI will look the same.

<PullQuote>

The economy does not need one god-model. It needs a million Stockfishes.

</PullQuote>

Electricity gave us thousands of appliances, each turning the same current into a particular service: a cold room, a clean shirt, light to read by. AI will take a similar shape. A legal-review system helps someone follow a contract. A scheduling system helps a workplace organise its day. Each needs its own rules and tests. The model supplies judgement; the surrounding software gives it a job it can do reliably.

The scaffolding also constrains failure. Permissions limit what an agent may touch. Logs record what it did. Rollback can restore software state, though it cannot undo every consequence in the world.

A frontier model teaches a smaller model the job. A compiler, schema check, or domain scorer tests the result. The specialist inherits much of its teacher's accuracy at a fraction of the cost. This is what the older hardware will host.

The frontier discovers capability. The plateau distributes it.

Distillation makes models smaller. Quantisation lets them run with fewer bits. Better software extracts more useful work from the same silicon (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-e">Appendix E</a>).<sup><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup> The chip sitting on a pallet, waiting for power, will have more it can do when it finally wakes.

<PullQuote>

Hardware depreciates. Algorithms compound.

</PullQuote>

<span id="vii-the-asymmetry"></span>

## VII. The Chip Ages. Its Uses Grow.

For the first owner, time destroys value. For the second, it creates it. During the years a chip loses its frontier place, the world learns to run smaller models, cheaper inference, and useful work that did not exist when it was made.

The older chip needs to clear a practical threshold: the model fits in memory, answers quickly enough, and earns more than it costs to power. The frontier keeps the work that asks for more. The plateau serves the work that has enough.

Institutions take years to absorb a new capability. A school has to learn where an AI tutor helps, how teachers will use it, and what to do when it misunderstands. While people are learning to trust and use a capability, industry keeps making that level of intelligence cheaper.

<PullQuote>

Useful intelligence can outlive the hardware cycle that paid for it.

</PullQuote>

This is the intelligence endowment: capabilities that keep getting cheaper while we discover what to do with them. Each wave leaves more within reach. The endowment is practically inexhaustible.

<span id="viii-the-intelligence-layer"></span>

## VIII. Intelligence Becomes Part of the World

Every collapse in the price of computation has made room for uses that once cost too much. Business software. Video games. AI. Cheap bandwidth gave us streaming and video calls. Cheap intelligence will change what we think to ask of a machine.

In the laboratory, a search for a useful material can run while its researchers work on the next question. What used to be a study becomes a query. What used to be a query becomes a background process.

A personal agent runs on your own device, without sending each question to a remote server. A child can ask a tutor the question that comes after the lesson, and the one after that.

*[Create an Age of Wonders](/essays/create-an-age-of-wonders)* named this: intelligence as infrastructure.<sup><a href="#ref-16">[16]</a></sup> Refrigeration, plumbing, roads. Each began as an event and became part of the world, present and taken for granted. Compute is in the middle of that transition. Intelligence is following it.

<Figure
  src={caDataCenter}
  alt="Rows of server racks in a datacentre lit in deep blue, receding into the dark"
  caption="Compute cheap enough to become part of everyday life."
  wide={true}
/>

Paul David showed it took American factories forty years after electrification to put a motor on every machine. The productivity payoff arrived when the factories were rebuilt around what electricity could do.<sup><a href="#ref-17">[17]</a></sup> AI is at the start of the same long arc, on a faster clock.

The AI bubble is the price of making intelligence accessible.

The first financiers inherit the loss.

**Civilisation inherits ambient intelligence.**

### <span id="appendix-d" class="appendix-anchor"></span>D. Energisation and Utilisation

*Capacity must first receive power, then find useful work.*

The energisation and utilisation figures concern different samples and stages. They cannot be multiplied:

- **Energisation share** describes ordered facility load that has been turned on. Source: public sample of named hyperscaler and sovereign projects in <a class="xref" href="#appendix-c">Appendix C</a>. Roughly a fifth of disclosed ordered load in the sample is plugged in.

Frontier training clusters likely run at much higher utilisation. The named projects cover only part of accelerator orders. Each sample exposes a different gap between purchased capacity and productive work.

The Cast AI 2026 State of Kubernetes Optimization Report shows the broader pattern: 8% average sustained CPU utilisation, 20% memory utilisation, 69% CPU overprovisioning headroom, 79% memory overprovisioning headroom, and fewer than 2% of GPU workloads on spot instances, primarily because spot capacity was effectively unavailable at hyperscaler scale. Specialised clusters using GPU time-slicing and MIG partitioning sustain up to 49% GPU utilisation.

### <span id="appendix-e" class="appendix-anchor"></span>E. Cost-Per-Token Decline

*Training capex and inference opex have diverged by orders of magnitude.*

Stanford HAI AI Index and Epoch AI compute-cost time series document a roughly 10× annual decline in inference cost at fixed capability level across the 2022–2026 window.<sup><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup>

GPT-3.5-equivalent capability cost ~\$20 per million tokens in late 2022 and ~\$0.07 per million tokens in late 2024, a roughly 280× reduction over two years. GPT-4 launched at \$30/\$60 per million input/output tokens. GPT-4o offered the same capability tier at \$3/\$10 per million eighteen months later. DeepSeek V3 entered at \$0.14 per million input tokens, undercutting GPT-4o by ~95%. Epoch AI reports a median annual decline of ~50× across the inference market.

Hardware, better algorithms, competition from open weights, and a maturing software stack all reduce inference cost. Quantisation, distillation, sparsity, speculative decoding, KV-cache optimisation, MoE routing, FlashAttention, and compilers contribute. Software and competition account for much more of the headline decline than hardware alone.

Large MoE training concentrates spending on synchronous power, interconnects, and coordination, with substantial instability risk. Memory-bound inference can use cheaper silicon, reading only the active experts per token. Appendix H examines where that advantage holds.

### <span id="appendix-f" class="appendix-anchor"></span>F. The Grid-Avoidance Tax

Grid queues have encouraged on-site gas generation. In 2026, Blackstone Tactical Opportunities and Halliburton invested \$1 billion in VoltaGrid, supporting a 7.5 GW order book through 2030 and manufacturing capacity of up to 300 MW per month. Modular reciprocating engines and turbines promise power in months under simplified minor-source air permits.<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#ref-25">[25]</a></sup>

Earlier power carries a recurring fuel cost.

Thermal efficiency of a generator is the ratio of its electrical output to fuel input, conventionally expressed via the operating heat rate (Btu of fuel per kWh of electricity):

$$
\eta = \frac{3{,}412.142}{\mathrm{HR}}
$$

Using the U.S. EIA's 2024 average tested heat rates by prime mover:<sup><a href="#ref-19">[19]</a></sup>

| Generation class | Heat rate (Btu/kWh) | Thermal efficiency | Gas consumption (ft³/kWh) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Combined-cycle utility gas | 7,548 | ~45.2% | ~7.35 |
| Average U.S. grid natural gas | 7,754 | ~44.0% | ~7.55 |
| Reciprocating internal-combustion gas | 8,924 | ~38.2% | ~8.69 |
| Simple-cycle gas turbine | 10,999 | ~31.0% | ~10.71 |

Gas consumption assumes approximately 1,027 Btu per cubic foot of pipeline natural gas.

The combined-cycle plant captures exhaust heat through a steam turbine. Modular simple-cycle and reciprocating units trade that recovery for speed and simpler installation. In the table above, the simple-cycle turbine consumes roughly 45.7% more natural gas per kWh than the combined-cycle plant, and emits CO₂ in direct proportion.

The 45.7% additional fuel is the grid-avoidance tax: a recurring premium for installing simple-cycle power sooner.

The tax compounds the regional cost spreads of <a class="xref" href="#appendix-b">Appendix B</a>. Where imported gas already costs six or seven times the Henry Hub price, a simple-cycle installation can run at nine to ten times the fuel cost of a Henry Hub combined-cycle plant. Getting power sooner means burning more fuel for every computation.

### <span id="appendix-g" class="appendix-anchor"></span>G. Slot Density, the OpEx Paradox, and the Migration Path

If every powered slot were equally scarce, its operator would choose the newest, most efficient chip. Even free older hardware would carry the opportunity cost of displacing that chip. This is the **OpEx versus slot paradox**.

**Contiguous power is scarce.** Synchronous training needs hundreds of megawatts or gigawatts at one permitted site, with cooling, deep networking, and experienced operators. Transformer queues, substation permits, and grid connections restrict the supply.

**Smaller powered sites are widespread.** Colocation facilities, university and sovereign clusters, industrial sites, laboratories, offices, and homes have fragmented capacity. Some can host useful work without expanding the grid.

| Slot tier | Power density | Site count (order of magnitude) | Workload type | Binding constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frontier | 100 MW – 1 GW contiguous | tens to low hundreds globally | Synchronous training | Substations, transformer queues, multi-year permitting |
| Plateau | 1 MW – 100 MW distributed | thousands | Inference, fine-tuning, batch, simulation | Existing colocation, sovereign, and enterprise capacity |
| Edge | 0.5 kW – 1 MW fragmented | millions | Local inference, agents, batch | Wall socket, room cooling, residential grid |

At a \$30,000–40,000 purchase price, an accelerator needs high utilisation and valuable work. Running a new chip at 15% utilisation in a 50 kW office slot can be uneconomic.

Depreciation lowers the utilisation needed to repay the purchase. A written-down chip can earn its place at 20% utilisation in a smaller slot that could not justify new hardware. Migration can open previously uneconomic sites as well as replace chips in existing ones.

A **follow-the-sun virtual cloud** could route latency-tolerant inference, fine-tuning, batch processing, and simulation to regions with cheap energy. Such work can use distributed capacity without a synchronous gigawatt-scale interconnect.

Verification in decentralised multi-tenant systems and WAN bandwidth remain constraints. Cheap hardware and available power create an incentive to solve them; the thesis does not assume they are already solved.

### <span id="appendix-h" class="appendix-anchor"></span>H. The Plateau Crossover

The crossover occurs when depreciation compensates for lower performance on the dimension a workload needs. Begin with purchase cost, then add software compatibility, memory fit, and operation.

**Definitions.** Four rates describe hardware growth, software improvement, depreciation, and workflow discovery; $\mu$ corrects for architectural incompatibility:

| Symbol | Quantity | Value or assumption | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| $g_q$ | Annual frontier hardware capability growth **per chip**, on the binding dimension (FLOPS/chip, GB/s/chip, FLOPS/W, etc., **not** capability-per-dollar) | Per-chip values from H100 SXM5 → B200 SXM, 3-yr annualised: ~1.31× (FP16/BF16 throughput); ~1.34× (memory bandwidth); ~1.34× (memory capacity, 80→192 GB); ~1.47× (FLOPS/W); ~1.65× (FP8 throughput) | Nvidia published specs; per-chip values, not per-dollar |
| $r_s$ | Annual software-efficiency multiplier at fixed capability | ~50× (inference cost-per-token); ~3× (pre-training compute-per-capability) | Stanford HAI AI Index; Epoch AI |
| $\delta$ | Annual secondary-market capital cost decay | ~0.25 (used H100 SXM5: ~\$35K new 2023 → ~\$15K used mid-2026) | Industry pricing |
| $\mu(t,\tau) \geq 1$ | Architectural divergence penalty (software gains hard-coded to new silicon that does not backport cleanly) | Modest for FP16/FP8 inference; significant for FP4 and the latest attention/decoding kernels | ISA differences |
| $\beta \geq 0$ | Workflow-discovery recursion rate (AI-assisted engineering productivity feedback) | Empirically positive; bounded above by deployment friction | This essay |

Use **physical capability per chip** for $g_q$; prices enter separately. Epoch's roughly 1.37× annual FLOP/s-per-dollar improvement combines per-chip capability and launch prices. Substituting it for $g_q$ would count price twice. Each workload needs its own binding dimension.

**The CapEx Crossover Identity.** Let $\tilde{C}(t, \tau)$ denote useful capability per dollar of a chip purchased used at time $t$, with age $\tau$:

$$
\tilde{C}(t, \tau) = \frac{Q_0 \cdot E(t)}{P_0 \cdot (1-\delta)^\tau}
$$

where $Q_0$ is the chip's physical capability on the binding dimension (fixed at manufacture) and $E(t)$ is the contemporary software multiplier. A new frontier chip at time $t$—assuming approximately stable launch prices across generations (the $p_F$ correction is treated below)—has capability $Q_0 \cdot g_q^\tau$ and price $P_0$:

$$
\tilde{C}_F(t) = \frac{Q_0 \cdot g_q^\tau \cdot E(t)}{P_0}
$$

The software multiplier $E(t)$ benefits both equally and cancels in the ratio. The structural factor—the **CapEx Crossover Identity**—is:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\tilde{C}(t, \tau)}{\tilde{C}_F(t)} = \frac{1}{[g_q \cdot (1-\delta)]^\tau}}
$$

When $g_q(1-\delta) < 1$, plateau hardware delivers more useful capability per dollar than frontier hardware on the binding dimension, with an exponentially growing advantage in $\tau$. This is a CapEx-only object. Operating costs enter in §H.5 below.

**The threshold condition.**

$$
g_q \cdot (1-\delta) < 1 \iff \delta > \frac{g_q - 1}{g_q}
$$

The secondary market must reprice faster than per-chip frontier capability advances on the dimension at hand. The condition is dimension-specific:

| Binding capability dimension (per chip, H100→B200, 3-yr annualised) | $g_q$ | Threshold $\delta^*$ | Observed $\delta$ | Identity fires? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP16/BF16 throughput per chip | 1.31 | 0.24 | 0.25 | Yes (marginal) |
| Memory bandwidth per chip | 1.34 | 0.25 | 0.25 | Yes (marginal) |
| Memory capacity per chip | 1.34 | 0.25 | 0.25 | Yes (marginal) |
| FLOPS per Watt per chip | 1.47 | 0.32 | 0.25 | No |
| FP8 throughput per chip | 1.65 | 0.39 | 0.25 | No |

At the rounded reference values, the purchase-cost advantage is marginal on bandwidth, capacity, and FP16/BF16 throughput. FP8 and FLOPS/W favour newer silicon. Rising launch prices change those thresholds.

**The launch-price correction.** Flagship retail prices have risen at $p_F \approx 1.05$/yr (H100 SXM5 ~\$30–35K in 2023, B200 SXM ~\$40K in 2025). Including this in the derivation: a plateau chip launched $\tau$ years ago had launch price $P_0/p_F^\tau$, and its used price today is $(P_0/p_F^\tau)(1-\delta)^\tau$. The full identity:

$$
\frac{\tilde{C}(t, \tau)}{\tilde{C}_F(t)} = \left[\frac{p_F}{g_q(1-\delta)}\right]^\tau
$$

with crossover condition:

$$
\delta > \frac{g_q - p_F}{g_q}
$$

Rising launch prices lower the depreciation threshold:

| Binding capability dimension | $g_q$ | Threshold $\delta^*$ ($p_F = 1.05$) | Observed $\delta$ | Identity fires? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP16/BF16 throughput per chip | 1.31 | 0.20 | 0.25 | Yes |
| Memory bandwidth per chip | 1.34 | 0.22 | 0.25 | Yes |
| Memory capacity per chip | 1.34 | 0.22 | 0.25 | Yes |
| FLOPS per Watt per chip | 1.47 | 0.29 | 0.25 | No (marginal) |
| FP8 throughput per chip | 1.65 | 0.36 | 0.25 | No |

Rising launch prices strengthen the first three comparisons. FP8 and FLOPS/W still favour the frontier at these assumptions.

**Numerical validation.** For mid-2026 used H100 (\$15K, 3.35 TB/s, age 3 yr) versus new B200 (\$40K, 8.0 TB/s, retail) on memory bandwidth, the full identity predicts:

$$
\frac{\tilde{C}_P}{\tilde{C}_F} = \left[\frac{p_F}{g_q(1-\delta)}\right]^\tau = \left[\frac{1.05}{1.34 \times 0.753}\right]^3 = \left[\frac{1.05}{1.009}\right]^3 = (1.041)^3 \approx 1.12
$$

The annualised prediction is about 12%; Table H.2 gives 11.5% directly (0.223 versus 0.200 GB/s per dollar). These are two calculations of the same price-to-bandwidth comparison. The next question is how much capability the job needs.

**H.1: Log capability scaling.** Inference benchmark scores scale approximately as $\beta_K \log_{10}(\text{compute})$. A 4× FLOPS deficit corresponds to a benchmark gap of roughly 3–5 percentage points on MMLU, GPQA, or HumanEval at current scale. Many useful workloads have a capability threshold: a customer-support router designed around 85% intent-classification accuracy can buy the cheapest model that clears it. As $E(t)$ grows, older hardware clears more of those thresholds.

**H.2: Memory-bound, not compute-bound.** Autoregressive LLM inference is memory-bandwidth-bound, not FLOPS-bound. The relevant cap-per-dollar metric is bandwidth per dollar:

| Hardware | State | Price | HBM bandwidth | GB/s per \$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H100 SXM5 | Retail (2023) | \$35,000 | 3.35 TB/s | 0.096 |
| H100 SXM5 | Used (mid-2026) | \$15,000 | 3.35 TB/s | **0.223** |
| B200 SXM | Retail (mid-2026) | \$40,000 | 8.0 TB/s | 0.200 |

Used H100 delivers ~12% more memory bandwidth per dollar than a new B200 at retail. The B200's structural advantages—192 GB VRAM, 4× FP8 throughput, NVLink 5 at 1.8 TB/s—bind on training workloads with synchronous gradient sync and on models exceeding 80 GB. Most production inference exhibits neither. Plateau hardware does not beat frontier globally. It beats frontier on the dimensions that govern specific deployment workloads.

**Latent recursion.** Research architectures such as COCONUT, looped transformers, and HRM/TRM reuse a small block across reasoning steps. Greater depth without proportionally more weights could help older chips clear H.1's capability thresholds.

A fixed latent state with a flat KV cache shifts the constraint towards serial latency and compute efficiency. Sparse routing, proposed as a remedy for representation collapse in dense looped blocks, can instead require repeated HBM reads. These regimes favour different hardware.

The proposed frontier core keeps a compact working set near compute, following the Groq/Cerebras direction. A distilled, weight-streaming MoE descendant may remain limited by memory bandwidth per dollar. The first favours new hardware; the second can favour the plateau.

This is a predicted architecture split, not an established production trend. Test each design by the resource its workload makes scarce.

**H.3: Architectural Divergence Penalty.** Software gains need not transfer equally. Blackwell's native FP4 and newer memory hierarchies can leave Hopper paying an emulation or compatibility cost. Let $\mu(t,\tau)\geq1$ represent that penalty:

$$
\frac{\tilde{C}(t, \tau)}{\tilde{C}_F(t)} = \frac{1}{\mu(t, \tau) \cdot [g_q \cdot (1-\delta)]^\tau}
$$

$\mu$ offsets the depreciation advantage. The penalty is expected to be modest for portable FP16/BF16/FP8 inference and larger for newer formats. Sustained lockout across useful workloads would defeat the migration thesis; distillation, quantisation, and portable kernels work against it.

**H.4: VRAM step-function.** A 70B-parameter model at FP8 occupies ~70 GB before KV cache. On H100 (80 GB), it fits with little headroom and typically requires two-GPU tensor-parallel configurations, introducing all-reduce communication overhead of 9–23% of end-to-end decoding latency. On H200 (141 GB) or B200 (192 GB), the same model fits single-GPU with concurrent KV caches and no TP penalty. The CapEx identity assumes capability decays smoothly. In reality, model-fit thresholds create non-linear cliffs. Plateau hardware wins inside the cliff and loses outside it. The migration thesis holds because most production workloads fit inside the cliff for previous-generation hardware, even as frontier *training* requires the next cliff.

**H.5: The Economic Crossover (OpEx-extended).** The first identity compares purchase costs. The full comparison also needs running costs. For a workload meeting a common quality threshold, define amortised capital cost per useful operation as $A(\tau, t, s, w)$ and operating cost per useful operation as $O(\tau, t, s, w)$, where $s$ indexes the slot and $w$ the workload. Economic capability per dollar:

$$
\tilde{C}_{\text{econ}}(\tau, t, s, w) = \frac{1}{A(\tau, t, s, w) + O(\tau, t, s, w)}
$$

Both costs are in dollars per useful operation, so their reciprocal gives useful operations per dollar. Throughput, software efficiency, architectural penalties and utilisation already enter the allocation of those costs over useful work. The comparison includes only configurations where the model clears the capability threshold, fits in memory, and has software support.

Electricity sets one operating floor. For a matched workload and accounting interval, revenue per token below the electricity cost per token cannot cover even that part of the running cost:

$$
R_{\text{token}} \;<\; \frac{\mathrm{PUE} \cdot P_{\mathrm{IT,kW}} \cdot P_{\text{elec,site}}}{3600 \cdot \mathrm{TPS}}
$$

Here $R_{\text{token}}$ is in \$/token, $P_{\mathrm{IT,kW}}$ is average IT power allocated to the workload in kilowatts, $\mathrm{PUE}$ allocates facility overhead, and $P_{\text{elec,site}}$ is the site's electricity cost in \$/kWh. Power and token throughput $\mathrm{TPS}$ must cover the same interval, including any idle energy charged to the workload. Thermal design power can serve as a planning proxy; measured operating draw closes the comparison. The factor of 3600 converts hours to seconds.

*Sanity check.* A workload averaging 1 kW of IT power at $\mathrm{PUE} = 1.2$, \$0.10/kWh electricity, and 100 TPS yields:

$$
\frac{(1.2)(1)(0.10)}{3600 \cdot 100} \;\approx\; 3.3 \times 10^{-7} \; \text{\$/token}
$$

—or roughly \$0.33 per million tokens for electricity alone.

The heat-rate penalty in <a class="xref" href="#appendix-f">Appendix F</a> raises the fuel needed per kilowatt-hour. It multiplies the fuel component of generation cost, not an entire utility tariff. Grid charges, fuel prices, plant costs and utilisation determine the actual site comparison. This is the link to plateau migration: written-down silicon still needs an affordable place to work. A university cluster, enterprise datacentre or regional cloud can offer one; its electricity bill and workload decide.

**H.6: The workflow-discovery recursion.** AI-assisted engineering can accelerate $E(t)$ by adapting software to available hardware. The prediction is $\beta>0$. The purchase-cost identity itself depends on shared software gains cancelling, rather than on their growth rate.

A stronger illustrative result follows if engineering throughput $L(t)$ scales with deployed plateau intelligence, $L(t) = L_0 \cdot E(t)$. Define the rate of software-efficiency improvement as:

$$
\frac{d \log E}{dt} = \alpha_0 + \beta \log L(t)
$$

Substituting the recursion and solving the resulting linear ODE in $u = \log E$:

$$
E(t) = E_\infty \cdot \exp\!\big(A \, e^{\beta t}\big)
$$

This illustrative double exponential suggests how software feedback might accelerate adoption. Verification, testing, integration, and organisational change constrain it. It is not a forecast; the weaker prediction is positive feedback.

**H.7: Frontier slowdown.** The $g_q$ values above assume the frontier continues to compound at recent rates. Three constraints suggest the rate may not hold indefinitely.

Moore's law has stalled at the device level. The frontier still compounds, but through capital intensity, packaging innovation, and system-scale engineering rather than transistor scaling. Hyperscaler AI spending exceeds \$700 billion in 2026.<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> Frontier training compute has grown ~5×/year since 2020, but hardware FLOP/\$ has improved only ~1.37×/year. Capability appears to scale approximately logarithmically with compute on standard benchmarks, so each additional order of magnitude buys a roughly fixed absolute increment, not a proportional one.

**Slower frontier growth lowers the threshold.** The illustrative $g_q=2.0$ per-release case and $g_q\approx1.15$ annual case use different intervals; comparisons must match them to depreciation. Continuing progress supplies new capabilities to distil. Slower progress can extend the useful life of existing capabilities.

**H.8: Boom-driven input-cost inflation.** Scarce HBM, packaging, substrates, networking, and power delivery raise new-hardware costs. The launch-price factor $p_F$ carries that pressure into the crossover.

Epoch AI's teardown places B200 module production cost at roughly \$5,700–7,300, with HBM and advanced packaging accounting for ~two-thirds of variable unit cost.<sup><a href="#ref-26">[26]</a></sup> SemiAnalysis estimates memory could rise to ~30% of hyperscaler AI data-centre capex in 2026, up from ~8% in 2023–2024, with HBM undersupplied through 2027.<sup><a href="#ref-27">[27]</a></sup> If boom-driven inflation pushes $p_F$ from 1.05 to 1.08 over the next two generations, the threshold on FLOPS/W (currently failing at 0.29 against observed $\delta = 0.25$) drops to ~0.26, on the cusp of firing. The wedge does not need the frontier to slow—only to keep getting more expensive.

**Committed supply.** Order books extend through Rubin and Rubin Ultra in 2027–2028 and Feynman in 2028–2029. The thesis expects a bust to reprice that committed pipeline faster than it changes delivery schedules. New and pre-ordered generations face the same slot economics.

**Three depreciation regimes.** The identity rests on distinguishing three depreciation rates that, in AI hardware, move at different speeds:

| Regime | Driven by | Timescale | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frontier obsolescence | $g_q$, $\mu$ | ~2 years | Chip no longer optimal for the next-largest frontier training run |
| Capability obsolescence | Software stack abandonment, physical failure | 5–10 years | Chip cannot run any useful workload |
| Economic obsolescence | OpEx vs revenue (slot $s$, workload $w$, site electricity cost) | Set by slot, workload, and electricity price | Output worth less than operating cost |

**Three empirical tests.** Keep the dates and observations distinct:

**Resale, end-2027.** B200 SXM should clear at \$22–28K, consistent with roughly 25–35% annual depreciation over two to three years. Prices above \$35K into 2028 would contradict the predicted repricing mechanism and its empirical crossover.

**Deployment, end-2028.** Hopper and early Blackwell should carry the largest share of useful AI work, assessed through tokens, queries, decisions, and dollars saved. A majority remaining on contemporary frontier silicon would defeat the workload prediction.

**Access pricing, end-2028.** The hyperscaler/specialist spread on identical H100/H200 hardware should remain wide or widen from a current maximum near 10×. Convergence below approximately 2× would defeat the predicted separation of premium access from the cheaper substrate, and the associated revenue/workload-share claim. Check public prices monthly. <a class="xref" href="#appendix-j"><a class="xref" href="#appendix-j">Appendix J</a></a>

Where repricing survives compatibility penalties and running costs, the second buyer inherits useful capacity at a price the first buyer could not obtain.

### <span id="appendix-i" class="appendix-anchor"></span>I. The Cascade in Progress

Older generations already serve different kinds of work at different prices. A bust makes that migration larger and more visible.

**Generation-by-generation status, mid-2026.**

| Generation | Launch | Launch price (top SKU) | Secondary clearing (mid-2026) | Current production role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turing T4 | 2018 | ~\$2,500–10,000 depending on SKU | ~\$1,500–2,500 | Default inference on AWS g4dn, GCP equivalent SKUs; embeddings, ranking, smaller-model serving across enterprise |
| Ampere A100 | 2020 | ~\$15,000–20,000 (40 / 80 GB SXM4) | ~\$5,000–8,000 | Primary production workload on AWS p4d, Azure ND A100, GCP a2; substantial share of enterprise AI inference globally |
| Hopper H100 | 2022/2023 | ~\$30,000–35,000 (SXM5) | ~\$15,000 (per <a class="xref" href="#appendix-h">Appendix H</a>) | Current workhorse; frontier-adjacent; per Table H.2 already trades at higher memory-bandwidth-per-dollar than new B200 retail |
| Hopper H200 | 2024 | ~\$30,000–40,000 (limited disclosed) | Limited secondary supply | Current production tier; increasingly inference-priced as Blackwell capacity ramps |
| Blackwell B200 | 2024/2025 | ~\$40,000 | Negligible secondary supply | Current frontier tier; first observable depreciation cycle still 12–24 months ahead |

**Cloud SKU repricing as the dominant migration channel.** Public AWS pricing data shows g4dn (T4) on-demand prices have fallen roughly 60–65% relative to 2019 launch; p4d (A100) by roughly 40–55% relative to 2021 launch. The chips never left Amazon. The migration ran entirely inside the hyperscaler's balance sheet, visible only as falling unit prices on customer-facing SKUs—the quiet form of the cascade the body describes.

<a class="xref" href="#appendix-j">Appendix J</a> records the second price surface: hyperscaler versus specialist access to the same hardware. Its spread measures the service bundle as well as chip economics.

**Hyperscaler depreciation extensions.** Microsoft (2022), Alphabet (2023), Meta (2023), Oracle (2024), and Amazon (2024) have each extended useful-life assumptions for server infrastructure from approximately four years to six years. Their audited accounts now assume a longer working life for the fleet. That expectation supports the distinction between leaving the frontier and ceasing to be useful.

Microsoft's FY2023 extension alone added ~\$3.7 billion to annual operating income. The aggregate impact across the top five hyperscalers is on the order of \$15 billion per year in reduced depreciation expense. The same CFOs are simultaneously issuing record bond volumes to fund new silicon and extending useful-life on the existing fleet. Together they imply that the productive compute fleet is becoming both larger and longer-lived than the original capacity model assumed.

**Falsification test on depreciation policy.** The cleanest forward signal against the plateau thesis would be a hyperscaler *shortening* AI-GPU depreciation in audited filings. As of mid-2026, no major hyperscaler has done so. The keynote curve makes capability claims unaudited. The audited curve makes utility claims unmarketed. The thesis tracks the second.

**Anthropic / Colossus as cascade in miniature.** In May 2026, Anthropic took over the entire ~290 MW capacity of Colossus 1, the Memphis facility built around 220,000+ Nvidia processors. The SpaceX S-1 disclosed an agreement at \$1.25 billion per month through May 2029, with reduced ramp fees and a 90-day termination right for either party.<sup><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup> xAI shifted internal frontier training to Colossus 2 in Southaven, Mississippi, deploying next-generation GB200 Blackwell. The older facility acquired a new tenant while its builder moved on.

**Abilene's change of hands.** The planned Oracle/OpenAI Stargate expansion from 1.2 GW to 2.0 GW collapsed in early 2026 amid financing complexity, revised OpenAI demand forecasts, and a winter weather event that took the liquid-cooling loop offline for several days. Microsoft took over the adjacent expansion with Crusoe, adding two buildings and an on-site 900 MW gas plant alongside Oracle's existing 350 MW backup.<sup><a href="#ref-14">[14]</a></sup>

**Cost-per-token compression as observable substrate-level repricing.** Cost-per-token at GPT-3.5-equivalent capability fell ~280× over 2022–2024 (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-e">Appendix E</a>). The decline is software-dominant. But the hardware-side decay rate is independently visible in SKU prices on ageing silicon, tracking the $\delta \approx 0.25$ band the framework predicts.

### <span id="appendix-j" class="appendix-anchor"></span>J. Hyperscaler Premium and the Plateau Substrate

The same chip sells at different rental prices depending on the services bundled with it.

**Snapshot, May 2026.** Public pricing for H100-class capacity, by access tier:

| Access tier | Example | Per-H100 GPU-hour |
|---|---|---|
| AWS on-demand | p5.48xlarge (8× H100) | ~\$6.88 (\$55.04/hr ÷ 8) |
| AWS reserved (Capacity Blocks) | p5.48xlarge reserved | ~\$4.33 (\$34.61/hr ÷ 8) |
| Azure on-demand | ND H100 v5 class | ~\$12.29 |
| Specialist cloud, mid-band | CoreWeave, Lambda, Civo, Denvr | ~\$2.25–\$4.29 |
| Marketplace / spot network | vast.ai-class supply, GPUPerHour | ~\$1.25–\$2.75 |
| Owned hardware, amortised | New H100 PCIe ~\$25K; SXM ~\$35–40K | under \$1.50 at high utilisation |

The maximum spread is roughly 10×. It prices availability, support, procurement, billing, and trust alongside hardware. Some customers need that bundle; others can trade it for lower cost.

**What the spread implies.** A chip can leave the frontier while its cloud rental stays expensive. The H100 remained priced as scarce on hyperscaler clouds after Blackwell launched, while marketplace rates approached amortised ownership cost. AWS raised H200 Capacity Block prices by 15% in January 2026.<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> The premium buys a ready-to-use slot as much as a chip.

**The bifurcation, mapped.** The premium access layer captures: largest training runs, premium managed inference, latency-sensitive global APIs, regulated enterprise workloads, customers paying for procurement simplicity, organisations without internal MLOps depth. The economic substrate captures: batch inference, embeddings, fine-tuning, document processing, simulation, scientific computing, bounded agentic systems, retrieval and ranking pipelines, narrow-vertical Stockfish workflows. Both surfaces persist. They serve different customers.

**Revenue and workload can separate.** Hyperscalers could retain most revenue through the enterprise premium while lower-cost providers serve most tokens, queries, or useful inference-hours. The prediction concerns both shares, not revenue alone.

**Multi-model routing volume as workload-share signal.** OpenRouter—the multi-model routing platform serving 8M+ developers—disclosed token throughput growing from ~5 trillion to ~25 trillion tokens per week over the six months ending May 2026, a 5× expansion across a heterogeneous plateau-tier model surface (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, DeepSeek, and open-weights).<sup><a href="#ref-28">[28]</a></sup> CapitalG led its \$113M Series B with NVIDIA Ventures, Snowflake, Databricks, MongoDB, ServiceNow, a16z, and Menlo Ventures—the enterprise-data and silicon stacks underwriting the multi-model topology rather than a winner-take-all frontier outcome. The data confirms the *topology* the framework predicts. Whether the absolute level is durable depends on how the 2026–2027 procurement reset resolves.

**Falsifier.** The same-hardware spread falling below approximately 2× by end-2028 defeats this prediction; it is the third test in H. The May 2026 snapshot shows widening. See <a class="xref" href="#appendix-h">Appendix H</a>.

**Moving prices.** Capacity Blocks, promotions, and spot markets reprice. The test concerns a durable spread on comparable hardware and service terms, not any single quoted rate.

**Cycle prediction, recorded July 2026.** The spread stays near 10× through the installation window, then compresses after repricing towards 2–3×. Falling below the 2× floor refutes the proposed bifurcation.

### <span id="appendix-k" class="appendix-anchor"></span>K. Macroeconomic Absorption and the Deployment-Phase Drag

The body argues that adoption takes years while hardware loses value in quarters. This appendix examines the gap between gains inside individual firms and gains across the economy. Brynjolfsson's Productivity J-Curve explains the work needed between buying a technology and using it well. Carlota Perez places that work in the longer history of infrastructure booms. My reading is a 5–10 year primary deployment phase, with the economy still absorbing the change beyond 2035.

#### <span id="appendix-k-1" class="appendix-anchor"></span>K.1. The Brynjolfsson J-Curve and intangible capital accumulation

Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson (2021) formalised why a massive general-purpose technology boom can co-exist with stagnant aggregate productivity.<sup><a href="#ref-29">[29]</a></sup> Firms must accumulate substantial intangible capital—process redesign, retraining, verification scaffolding, organisational restructuring—before the technology's productivity dividend appears in measured output. Because the intangible accumulation is expensed as opex (not capitalised), national accounts systematically under-measure both GDP and TFP during the absorption phase. Historical adjustments for computer-era intangibles found that true TFP was 15.9% higher than official measures by the end of 2017.

The J-Curve is now quantified in current AI data. PricewaterhouseCoopers' 2026 AI Performance Study (1,217 senior executives across 25 industries) finds 74% of AI's measurable economic value captured by the top 20% of firms, leaving 26% to the remaining 80%.<sup><a href="#ref-30">[30]</a></sup> The long tail of adopters has not yet completed the intangible-capital accumulation that would let them realise the gains, even where they have purchased the software.

The cash budget ratio is now visible. The SXSW 2026 CMO Survey of 400 organisations finds that positive-ROI AI deployments spent approximately \$2.60 on training and change management per \$1 spent on the AI software itself, with organisations failing to match this ratio experiencing tool abandonment rates of 60–70% within six months.<sup><a href="#ref-31">[31]</a></sup>

The implied market-value ratio is older but consistent. Brynjolfsson, Hitt, and Yang (2002) found \$1 of physical computer hardware historically associated with approximately \$9 of corporate market value—the market pricing the unmeasured complementary intangible capital long before the national accounts measured it.

#### <span id="appendix-k-2" class="appendix-anchor"></span>K.2. Enterprise deployment friction is structural, not transient

The deployment friction is not theoretical. S&P Global Market Intelligence and the RAND Corporation (2025) report that 42% of corporate AI projects were scrapped in 2025 and 80.3% of AI initiatives failed to deliver business value—twice the failure rate of traditional, non-AI IT projects.<sup><a href="#ref-32">[32]</a></sup> Gartner projects that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end-2027 due to escalating costs, lack of clear business value, and inadequate risk controls.<sup><a href="#ref-32">[32]</a></sup> Although 97% of surveyed enterprises have experimented with AI agents in some form, only 10–12% have successfully transitioned them into production environments.

At the workflow level, the downstream verification burden compounds the friction. The METR randomised controlled trial (2025) found experienced open-source developers were 19% *slower* using AI tools. The cognitive cost of reviewing non-deterministic AI output exceeded the speedup from generation.<sup><a href="#ref-33">[33]</a></sup>

Google's DORA 2024 report associated a 25% increase in AI adoption with a 7.2% *decrease* in production delivery stability. Google Cloud (2026) reports 45% higher burnout among frequent AI users, with approval fatigue identified as the primary mechanism. GitClear's analysis of 153 million changed code lines projected a doubling of code churn translating directly into delivery instability.

The proposed mechanism is that verification and integration absorb some isolated-task speedups, reported at 20–56%. These studies measure different tasks and outcomes; they do not supply one common estimate.

#### <span id="appendix-k-3" class="appendix-anchor"></span>K.3. The labour-market signal

The Stanford AI Index 2026 reports roughly 20% lower early-career software-developer employment (ages 22–25) in exposed roles from 2024 to early 2026, alongside stable aggregate white-collar wages. This is consistent with human work shifting towards verification, integration, and architecture.<sup><a href="#ref-34">[34]</a></sup>

The employment pattern does not identify that mechanism. Hiring, demand, and organisational change also matter. [Symbolic Superintelligence](/essays/symbolic-superintelligence) proposes testing what work remains and what makes the next task automatable.

#### <span id="appendix-k-4" class="appendix-anchor"></span>K.4. Aggregate productivity dispersion and the Perez modification

U.S. aggregate labour productivity has grown at 1.6% annualised since Q4 2019—a modest acceleration from the 1.2% pre-pandemic decade. Forward projections are widely dispersed.

Goldman Sachs Global Macro Research projects AI-driven labour productivity acceleration to 1.7–1.9% through 2029, peaking at 1.9–2.3% in the early 2030s, with potential GDP growth elevated to 2.1–2.3% for the rest of the decade.<sup><a href="#ref-35">[35]</a></sup>

Penn Wharton Budget Model projects permanent annual potential-output gains of less than 0.04 percentage points, with aggregate GDP rising only 1.5% by 2035.<sup><a href="#ref-36">[36]</a></sup> Acemoglu (2024) similarly estimates aggregate GDP gains of 1.1–1.6% over a decade and a marginal annual TFP boost of approximately 0.05%.<sup><a href="#ref-37">[37]</a></sup>

The dispersion reflects genuine uncertainty about how fast the J-Curve resolves.

Perez's installation → crash → deployment sequence separates speculative finance from production capital. After the telecom crash, consolidators such as Level 3 and Equinix acquired infrastructure cheaply and leased it into the next phase.

Hyperscalers combine those roles on one balance sheet. Cash flows and low-cost debt—\$121B of bonds in 2025, per Note [2]—can fund new equipment while older chips move internally to inference. In this scenario, clearing concentrates among specialist clouds and developers; access changes hands through powered slots and leases as well as equipment sales.

#### <span id="appendix-k-5" class="appendix-anchor"></span>K.5. Implications for the cascade

The adoption timetable changes the route to the plateau:

**Timing.** My estimate remains a 5–10 year primary deployment phase with further absorption beyond 2035. The evidence motivates a long adjustment; it does not measure that forecast.

**Demand.** In the optimistic Goldman case, rising workloads fill cheaper capacity sooner. In the Penn Wharton/Acemoglu case, slower adoption can extend the life of sufficient hardware. Both leave room for the plateau, provided useful demand covers its operating cost.

**Access.** Cheaper capacity lets firms experiment while they learn how to use the technology.

#### <span id="appendix-k-6" class="appendix-anchor"></span>K.6. A note on method

The predictions are dated and testable. A failed prediction changes the argument.

### <span id="appendix-m" class="appendix-anchor"></span>M. The Q1 2026 Register

These figures preserve what was reported at the time. Revenue, losses, and adoption may change; the dated record lets later readers test the essay against what followed. Sources include Reuters, Bloomberg, and The Information.<sup><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup>

- **OpenAI:** Q1 revenue ≈ \$5.7 billion against an operating loss ≈ \$6.95 billion (−122% margin), tracking toward a \$36.6 billion annual loss. ChatGPT weekly active users stalled at 905 million against a 1 billion target. Sora shut down. The Disney partnership ended. The Walmart pilot shuttered.
- **xAI:** \$2.47 billion Q1 loss on \$818 million of revenue—roughly \$1 billion a month of burn. Grok absent from the top-25 App Store downloads.
- **Anthropic:** \$30 billion annualised revenue run rate by April—80× annualised Q1 growth against a 10× target, most plausibly a short-run anomaly of coding-adoption scaling rather than a rate that holds the full year. Claude Code past \$1 billion ARR, on track for ≈ \$11 billion of Q2 revenue at ≈ \$600 million operating profit.

**Adoption.** Late-2025 surveys from Stack Overflow, JetBrains, and GitHub's Octoverse put professional-developer adoption of AI tools above 70%.<sup><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup> Growth increasingly depends on existing users doing more with the tools. Coding entered a field with compilers, tests, version control, and decades of deployment practice. Other fields are building their equivalents: NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, sector-specific evaluation suites, and agent-testing tools.<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup> Medicine and finance show how capability can precede the institutions needed to use it.<sup><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup>

**Falsifier.** The interpretation behind §II is that profitability follows the ability to put models to work. If, by 2028, the lab ranking by operating margin does not track the maturity of those deployment systems, the argument is wrong.
