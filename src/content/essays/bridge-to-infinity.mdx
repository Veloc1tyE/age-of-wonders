Imagine the journey.

Three g, sustained: a firm, even weight pressing you into the reclined seat. The cabin is small. Twelve people. Everyone breathing steadily against the load.

You are inside a mountain, accelerating through an evacuated shaft bored through bedrock. The coils fire in sequence. No roar. No vibration. Just the low hum of grid electricity becoming speed. Thirty seconds. Eight hundred and fifty metres per second.

The tunnel curves toward vertical. The push shifts sideways, centripetal, building. Three g becoming five, briefly touching seven at the tightest bend. Twenty seconds of roller-coaster geometry through the mountain's shoulder. Then it eases.

**And silence.**

The vehicle coasts up the pylon in vacuum. Sixteen seconds of weightlessness. Through the window, the sky is black. Stars steady against deep indigo. The curve of the Earth runs pale along the southeast horizon. Twenty kilometres up, the air outside is less than a tenth of what it was on the ground.

The exit is gentle. At seven hundred metres per second, the thin air presses softly, less dynamic pressure than a rocket ever faces at Max Q. The lifting body banks: one smooth roll, about one g. Then the engine lights: a nudge, under one g, building over seven minutes to five g in the final seconds as propellant burns off. Most of the burn is below three g. Quieter than turbulence.

Finally. Cutoff.

**Orbit.**

## I. The Sky We Leave Behind

To reach orbit, a spacecraft must gain speed while climbing through air.

A spacecraft in low Earth orbit moves at roughly 7.8 km/s. Fast enough to circle the planet in ninety minutes. But reaching orbit from the ground costs <a class="xref" href="#appendix-b">closer to 9.2 km/s</a>.<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> That extra 1.4 km/s is almost entirely wasted fighting gravity and air. These losses compound exponentially through the rocket equation.<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>

Max Q—the moment of greatest aerodynamic pressure—shapes the vehicle's shell, structure, and engine schedule. Rockets are built around ninety seconds of dense air.

<DataInsight
  insight="95% of Earth's atmospheric mass sits below 25 km."
  context="An elevated launcher would give the orbital stage a thinner atmosphere to cross."
/>

Reusable rockets will bootstrap the space economy, pushing costs toward \$50–200/kg and seeding the first lunar industry. But chemical rocketry through dense atmosphere faces a throughput ceiling that has nothing to do with cost. Every launch deposits combustion products, soot, aluminium oxide, water vapour, directly into the stratosphere, where they accumulate.

NOAA-affiliated research shows non-linear stratospheric ozone and climate damage at roughly 10× current launch rates.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> Measurements already detect spacecraft re-entry metals in stratospheric aerosol at today's cadence.<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> Moving civilisation off-Earth requires not 10× but 400–1,000× current orbital throughput, megatonnes per year, sustained for decades.

Rockets are the horse. The Bridge is the railroad.

<span id="ii-the-needle"></span>

## II. A Needle Through the Atmosphere

The proposal is infrastructure: a vertical electromagnetic launcher, embedded in a mountain, reaching through the stratosphere.

**Meridian.**

<Figure
  src={halcyonAngled}
  alt="Halcyon orbital vehicle banking through the upper atmosphere, angled view showing the lifting body geometry against a darkening sky"
  caption="Halcyon concept. The proposed bank turns part of the launcher's vertical throw towards the horizon."
  wide
/>

A tunnel accelerates the vehicle through bedrock. A pylon carries it from the summit into the stratosphere. At altitude, the lifting body banks, turning towards the horizon before the engine has burned a gram of propellant (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>, <a class="xref" href="#appendix-e">E</a>).

Twenty kilometres is where three conditions coincide. Thick enough for a lifting body to bank: <a class="xref" href="#appendix-a">7% of sea-level density</a> gives real aerodynamic authority at even modest speeds. Sparse enough that the exit pulse is brief and structurally manageable, gentler than re-entry, which is already solved. And high enough that the aerodynamic bank carries the vehicle to <a class="xref" href="#appendix-c">~28–29 km before the engine lights</a>.

At that altitude, atmospheric pressure is under 2% of sea level. The engine fires in effectively true vacuum from ignition, at 99%+ of its theoretical efficiency. No other exit altitude satisfies all three as cleanly.

The bank turns vertical speed towards the horizon. In the idealised geometry, gaining a hundred metres per second sideways costs about seven metres per second of climb speed. Call it the Pythagorean Dividend (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>). Drag still takes its share. The stratosphere becomes a gearbox, turning part of the launcher's vertical throw into speed the orbital stage can use.

Electricity provides the throw. Thin air provides the turn. Vacuum provides the burn. Each layer does less work because the one before it did more. The rocket equation amplifies every saving exponentially.

## III. The Mountain is a Stage

Once the atmosphere is someone else's problem, the orbital stage transforms.

Call it Halcyon. It ignites in near-vacuum, already banked toward the horizon. Its nozzle can expand fully for space. The same lifting body that turns through the thin air comes home to a runway, using the re-entry techniques that reusable spacecraft have developed (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-c3">Appendix C3</a>).<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>

And it can use hydrogen.

Ground vehicles default to dense propellants that survive Max Q. Hydrogen offers far higher exhaust velocity, but its bulk punishes sea-level flight. Meridian removes that constraint. The engine fires a pure vacuum nozzle at full expansion ratio. It is like the second stage of a two-stage rocket.<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>

In the cargo model, hydrogen and oxygen cut propellant by 64% against the all-rocket kerosene baseline; the gentler crewed flight saves 58% (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-d">Appendix D</a>). Halcyon's broad body gives the bulky hydrogen somewhere to go. Nuclear thermal propulsion offers a later upgrade (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-m">Appendix M</a>).<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>

Engineers have chased the spaceplane since the 1960s. A reusable single-stage-to-orbit vehicle must fit structure, propellant, and payload inside an unforgiving mass budget. The X-33 and VentureStar ran into that constraint (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-q">Appendix Q</a>). Meridian moves the starting line to 20 km. In the model, that leaves room for a reusable vehicle and useful cargo.

The mountain carries what the vehicle never could.

</PullQuote>


<span id="iv-the-port-the-locomotive-the-container"></span>

## IV. A Timetable to Orbit

The difference between a space programme and a space economy is cadence. A mature orbital economy needs the regularity of ports, rail, and container shipping.

**The port.** Meridian is fixed infrastructure, used constantly, maintained on schedules, amortised over decades. It never moves. Electricity is Meridian's main variable input. Vehicle propellant, maintenance, and amortisation set the delivered price.

**The locomotive.** Halcyon: a reusable lifting body, optionally crewed, that mates with Meridian, completes orbital insertion in near-vacuum, glides back to a runway, and turns around for the next flight.

**The container.** Standardised cargo pods, sized to the launcher bore and the shuttle bay. The container is cheap. The locomotive is expensive. You replace containers. You maintain locomotives.

The throughput changes the conversation. In 2024, all of humanity put roughly 2,171 tonnes into orbit.<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup> One launcher at daily cadence delivers more than ten times that. Faster cadences scale toward <a class="xref" href="#appendix-g">400× the world's annual upmass</a> at a shot an hour.

<span id="v-the-two-mass-drivers"></span>

## V. The Moon Supplies the Bricks

The Moon is the natural source of bulk mass for space.

The Moon's gravity well is 22 times shallower than Earth's.<sup><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup> The energy to lift a kilogram of structural aluminium from the Moon to cis-lunar orbit is less than <a class="xref" href="#appendix-h">one-twentieth the energy to lift the same kilogram from Earth</a>.<sup><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup> And the Moon has no atmosphere, no vacuum tube, no aerodynamic heating, no Max Q. A lunar mass driver is roughly 14 km of track at 20g under open sky, powered from crater rims where the Sun almost never sets.

Launching processed material from the Moon into cis-lunar orbit costs roughly <a class="xref" href="#appendix-j">\$0.04/kg in electricity</a>. Processing the regolith costs far more. The gravity well is the part that disappears.<sup><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup>

The Moon's crust is exactly the inventory a space civilisation needs for bulk construction: oxygen, silicon, aluminium, iron, magnesium, calcium, titanium.<sup><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup> Structural metals, glass, ceramics, radiation shielding, the heavy, voluminous things that dominate construction mass and are expensive to launch from Earth because there is so much of them.

The round-trip light time to the Moon is about 2.6 seconds: too slow for direct reflexes, fast enough for supervised teleoperation of industrial robots, excavators, and fabrication systems.<sup><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup> The first lunar industrial base runs on machines and a data link.

<PullQuote>

The Moon supplies the bricks. Electricity lifts them.

</PullQuote>

An O'Neill-scale rotating habitat for 10,000 people might mass 150,000 tonnes. If the Moon supplies 90% of that as structural bulk, Earth only needs to export roughly 15,000 tonnes per habitat, about 1.5 tonnes per person. As orbital fabs mature, the per-capita burden decays toward an irreducible floor of genuinely Earth-origin things: semiconductors, biological diversity, precision instruments, cultural capital. The full decay model is in <a class="xref" href="#appendix-l">Appendix L</a>.

Housing is one task; carrying people there is another. A passenger needs a vehicle, propellant, life support, and reserves. Reuse makes the journey cheaper over time. Cargo goes first, until the fare falls far enough that millions can choose to follow.

Earth exports the blueprint. The Moon exports the bricks. Cis-lunar space builds the city.

## VI. For a City Between Two Worlds

An economy needs supply chains that close, transactions that settle, and machines that take instructions. Across the Earth–Moon system, a signal returns in under three seconds: slow enough to notice, fast enough to coordinate. Beyond the Moon, the delay grows quickly. Cis-lunar space can still function as one economy because its machines, markets, and people can answer one another.<sup><a href="#ref-14">[14]</a></sup>

<PullQuote>

Meridian makes orbit cheap. Latency defines how far the economy stretches.

</PullQuote>


Inside that neighbourhood, industry migrates in a specific order.

Heavy industry goes first. Smelting, refining, and chemical processing generate heat and waste Earth's biosphere must absorb. In space, radiators discharge heat into the dark. Feedstock arrives from the Moon. Power follows industry. At the right high orbit, sunlight is nearly continuous. [Computation follows power](/essays/leviathan). Then workers. Then families. Then cities.

Each arrival depends on the one before it. Population follows closed logistics, the same way every city in history was a port or a crossroads before it was a home.


<span id="vii-the-great-de-industrialisation"></span>

<span id="vii-earth-has-room-to-breathe"></span>

## VII. The Earth Breathes

For three centuries, industrial civilisation has used Earth's atmosphere as a chimney, its rivers as coolant, its crust as a mine, and its ecosystems as a buffer against the side effects of production.

Meridian changes that.

Once heavy industry can operate in space, powered by continuous solar energy, fed by lunar material, cooled by radiators facing the void, the pressure on Earth's biosphere begins to reverse.

<Figure
  src={earthset}
  alt="Earthset photographed by the Artemis II crew, Earth setting below the lunar horizon"
  caption="Earthset, Artemis II, 2026. The world an orbital economy would leave behind."
  wide
/>

The atmosphere stops absorbing industrial waste heat. The oceans stop receiving runoff from extraction. Mines close because their products are better sourced from the Moon. Smelters, refineries, and chemical plants migrate to orbital platforms where their emissions no longer enter a living biosphere.

Space industry manufactures infrastructure for space residents: habitat shells, solar arrays, structural trusses, propellant tankage, radiator panels. The output stays in space because the customers are in space. At scale, the orbital economy consumes its own output to grow.

Earth becomes selective about what it hosts. Biology. Culture. Agriculture on land that was never strip-mined. Forests that were never cleared for industry. Rivers that run clean because no one is using them as coolant.

<DataInsight
  insight="The most ambitious conservation project in history could be an infrastructure programme."
  context="Give industry somewhere better to go."
/>

Earth needs civilisation to grow large enough to move its heaviest footprint somewhere else. Give it a larger canvas. The footprint shrinks.

Meridian has its own costs: solar land use, electrolysis water demand, re-entry aerosols, orbital debris governance. Each demands serious engineering (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-n">Appendix N</a>).<sup><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-16">[16]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-17">[17]</a></sup> But the sum of managed externalities at scale is categorically smaller than continuing to use Earth's biosphere as a process buffer.

<span id="viii-the-cathedral"></span>

## VIII. Building Our Cathedral

This will be a programme of infrastructure built across generations, the way cathedrals were built, by people who knew they would never see the finished work, for communities that did not yet exist.

Each phase depends on the one before it. Rockets bootstrap the lunar economy. The lunar economy justifies Meridian. Meridian feeds the orbital build-out. The build-out creates the demand that pulls industry, then populations, off-world. The chain ends with [the free starships](/essays/the-free-starship): vessels that can leave the supply line behind.

Once Meridian is built, the cost of orbit drops to electricity and process engineering. The Moon supplies the mass. Earth supplies only the complexity. Cis-lunar industry builds what cis-lunar residents need.

And Earth becomes what it always could have been.

**Our Pale Blue Dot.**

### <span id="appendix-a" class="appendix-anchor"></span>A. Atmospheric Density and the 95% Line

*The launcher exit dynamic pressure is high and useful.*

Using the U.S. Standard Atmosphere (1976),<sup><a href="#ref-18">[18]</a></sup> air density $\rho$ falls approximately exponentially with altitude:

| Altitude (km) | $\rho$ (kg/m³) | % of sea-level density | Cumulative mass above (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (sea level) | 1.225 | 100% | 100% |
| 10 | 0.414 | 33.8% | ~26% |
| 20 | 0.089 | 7.3% | ~5.5% |
| 25 | 0.039 | 3.2% | ~2.5% |
| 30 | 0.018 | 1.5% | ~1.2% |

Atmospheric pressure at altitude $h$ approximates the weight of the overlying air column: $P(h) = P_0 \, e^{-h/H}$, where $H \approx 8.5$ km is the scale height. Since pressure is proportional to the mass of air above, ~94% of atmospheric mass lies below 20 km, ~97% below 25 km, and ~99% below 30 km (the body's "95% below 25 km" is the conservative round).

Dynamic pressure at launcher exit: $q = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2$

| Exit altitude | $\rho$ (kg/m³) | $v$ = 2.5 km/s | $v$ = 3.0 km/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 km | 0.089 | 278 kPa | 401 kPa |
| 25 km | 0.039 | 122 kPa | 176 kPa |
| 30 km | 0.018 | 56 kPa | 81 kPa |

For comparison, a typical rocket experiences Max Q of 30–40 kPa at ~12 km altitude and ~500 m/s. A lower exit (20–25 km) exposes the vehicle to more air. More air means higher aerodynamic authority for the bank manoeuvre that converts surplus vertical velocity into horizontal momentum (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>).

A lower exit shortens the pylon and increases aerodynamic authority. At 20 km, the purpose-built cargo model gains about 500 m/s horizontally. Its 3 km/s climb carries it rapidly through the highest-pressure layer.


### <span id="appendix-b" class="appendix-anchor"></span>B. Launch Budget Decomposition

*The benefit of altitude is atmospheric bypass, not orbital mechanics.*

A representative NASA launch budget for a 200 km circular orbit:<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

| Component | $\Delta v$ (km/s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ideal surface-to-orbit | 7.97 | Vis-viva from surface to 200 km circular |
| Earth rotation credit | −0.41 | At 28.5° latitude (Cape Canaveral class) |
| Gravity loss | +1.10 | Propellant spent fighting gravity during ascent |
| Drag loss | +0.50 | Propellant spent overcoming aerodynamic drag |
| Steering loss | +0.10 | Trajectory shaping and guidance corrections |
| **Total rocket $\Delta v$** | **9.26** | |

Circular orbital speed at 200 km: $v_{circ} = \sqrt{\mu / (R_E + h)} \approx 7.79$ km/s.

**What the launcher removes.** A near-vertical electromagnetic assist to ~20 km altitude and ~3 km/s upward velocity eliminates or sharply reduces:
- Drag loss (~0.5 km/s): payload exits above 93%+ of the atmosphere
- Gravity loss (~0.9–1.1 km/s): all but eliminated. The launcher does the vertical climb electromagnetically (no engine burn, no gravity loss during ascent). The lifting body banks immediately after exit, converting vertical momentum into horizontal momentum aerodynamically before engine ignition, so the powered burn begins near-horizontal and nearly perpendicular to the gravity vector
- Steering loss (~0.1 km/s): the vacuum stage can pitch directly to optimal burn attitude
- *Additional*: the aerodynamic bank converts ~100–500 m/s of surplus vertical velocity into horizontal momentum at zero propellant cost, depending on vehicle class (~500 m/s for purpose-built cargo at 3 km/s, ~100 m/s for human-rated at 700 m/s with full bank. See <a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>)

The cargo budget removes roughly 1.5 km/s of launch losses and credits another 500 m/s to the bank, leaving about 7.2 km/s for the engine. The human case exits at 700 m/s, gains about 100 m/s aerodynamically, and assigns about 7.8 km/s to the engine. See <a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>.

**Altitude vs velocity contribution.** The change in required circular orbital speed due to the slightly larger orbital radius at 30 km is:

$$
\Delta v_{alt} = v_{circ}(0) - v_{circ}(30\text{ km}) = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{R_E}} - \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{R_E + 30}} \approx 19 \text{ m/s}
$$

The 19 m/s change is negligible beside the atmospheric and gravity-loss savings.


### <span id="appendix-c" class="appendix-anchor"></span>C. Ballistic Arc and Burn Window

These unpowered arcs define the coasting envelope. The intended stage banks and ignites seconds after exit, so its powered trajectory departs from the table almost immediately.

From an exit altitude $h_0$ with vertical velocity $v_0$, the additional height gained on an unburned arc:

$$
\Delta h = \frac{v_0^2}{2g}
$$

where $g \approx 9.8$ m/s² at 20 km (negligibly less than surface $g_0$).

| Exit altitude | $v_0$ (km/s) | Additional height | Apogee altitude | Time to apogee | Total time above exit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 km | 0.7 (human) | 25 km | 45 km | 71 s (1.2 min) | ~2.4 min |
| 20 km | 2.5 | 319 km | 339 km | 255 s (4.3 min) | ~9 min |
| 20 km | 3.0 (cargo) | 459 km | 479 km | 306 s (5.1 min) | ~10 min |
| 25 km | 3.0 | 459 km | 484 km | 306 s (5.1 min) | ~10 min |
| 30 km | 3.0 | 459 km | 489 km | 306 s (5.1 min) | ~10 min |

The 3 km/s cargo case has roughly ten minutes above its 20 km exit altitude without a burn. The intended bank gains about 500 m/s before ignition; the powered ascent uses that speed rather than following the full ballistic arc. See <a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>.

For a cargo hydrolox stage with $\Delta v \approx 7.2$ km/s (after ~500 m/s aero credit at 20 km), a 5-minute burn (300 s) gives average acceleration $\bar{a} = 7200 / 300 = 24$ m/s², or about 2.4 g—a reasonable upper-stage acceleration for cargo. The crewed vehicle burns for ~7 minutes from 0.9 g to 5 g (85% below 3 g); its 2.4-minute ballistic window is extended by wing lift and centrifugal effects, so the burn fits comfortably. See <a class="xref" href="#appendix-e">Appendix E</a>.


### <span id="appendix-c2" class="appendix-anchor"></span>C2. Aerodynamic Turn Optimisation

The cargo model supplies about 3 km/s vertically while reserving roughly 500–700 m/s to remain above the atmosphere during the burn. The bank converts part of the surplus to horizontal velocity. The following approximation estimates that conversion.

**Closed-form solution.** Air density falls exponentially with altitude: $\rho(h) = \rho_0 \, e^{-(h - h_0)/H}$ where $H \approx 7.5$ km is the scale height. For a vehicle climbing at velocity $v_0$, altitude increases as $h(t) \approx h_0 + v_0 t$, so $\rho(t) = \rho_0 \, e^{-v_0 t / H}$.

The lateral acceleration from aerodynamic lift is:

$$
a(t) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho(t) \, v_0^2 \, S \, C_L}{m} = \frac{\rho_0 \, v_0^2 \, S \, C_L}{2m} \, e^{-v_0 t / H}
$$

Integrating over the entire climb (the exponential decay makes the integral converge):

$$
\boxed{\Delta v_{\text{aero}} = \frac{\rho_0 \, v_0 \, H \, S \, C_L}{2m}}
$$

This is exact in the limit of constant climb speed (a good approximation: the vehicle loses only ~2–3% of its 3 km/s to gravity during the ~3 s of significant aero authority). Aero delta-v scales linearly with exit density $\rho_0$, exit speed $v_0$, scale height $H$, and the vehicle's lift parameter $S C_L / m$.

Since $\rho_0 = \rho_{\text{SL}} \, e^{-h_0/H}$, **every $H\ln 2 \approx 5$ km lower in exit altitude doubles the free delta-v** (each 7.5 km multiplies it by $e$). This is why exit altitude matters so much.

**Wing loading.** The formula uses full exit mass, including propellant. A 50 t payload with hydrolox for a 7.2 km/s burn gives roughly 484 t at exit.

Runway landing sets planform area through $W_L=m_{\text{final}}g/S$. With $m_{\text{exit}}=R\,m_{\text{final}}$, substitution gives:

$$
\boxed{\Delta v_{\text{aero}} = \frac{\rho_0 \, v_0 \, H \, C_L \, g}{2 \, R \, W_L}}
$$

**Absolute mass cancels.** Landing wing loading, lift coefficient, exit density, and mass ratio determine the result. A heavier vehicle requires proportionally more planform.

For a purpose-built cargo drone landing at $W_L \approx 2{,}000$ N/m² (approach speed ~130 knots, comparable to a regional airliner) with $C_L = 0.5$ at ~30° hypersonic AoA, carrying 50 t of payload and ~390 t of hydrolox at exit ($R \approx 4.8$): planform area ~490 m² (~31 m × 16 m), total exit mass ~484 t. At 20 km exit this gives ~500 m/s—half a kilometre per second of free horizontal velocity, earned from the air itself.

**Design envelope.** For a 50 t payload at $v_0 = 3$ km/s, hydrolox, $\varepsilon = 0.10$, solved self-consistently ($R$ and $\Delta v_{\text{aero}}$ co-determined):

| Exit altitude | $\rho_0$ (kg/m³) | $q_0$ (kPa) | Aero $\Delta v$ (conservative) | Aero $\Delta v$ (purpose-built) | Peak lateral $g$ (conservative) | Peak lateral $g$ (purpose-built) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 km | 0.195 | 878 | 527 m/s | 1,097 m/s | 21 g | 44 g |
| 18 km | 0.122 | 549 | 329 m/s | 686 m/s | 13 g | 28 g |
| 20 km | 0.089 | 401 | 240 m/s | 501 m/s | 10 g | 20 g |
| 22 km | 0.065 | 293 | 175 m/s | 366 m/s | 7 g | 15 g |
| 25 km | 0.039 | 176 | 105 m/s | 219 m/s | 4.3 g | 9 g |
| 28 km | 0.024 | 108 | 65 m/s | 135 m/s | 2.6 g | 5.5 g |
| 30 km | 0.018 | 81 | 49 m/s | 101 m/s | 2.0 g | 4.1 g |

Conservative: $W_L = 3{,}000$ N/m², $C_L = 0.3$, $L/D = 2.0$ (smaller planform, moderate bank). Purpose-built: $W_L = 2{,}000$ N/m², $C_L = 0.5$, $L/D = 1.5$ (large planform, aggressive bank). Both columns are self-consistent: $R$ is computed from the remaining stage duty after aero credit.

Peak lateral $g$ is the instantaneous load at exit, decaying to 37% within 2.5 seconds ($H/v_0$) as the vehicle climbs into thinner air. These are *brief* pulses, not sustained loads.

**G-load constraints and optimal exit altitude.** The peak lateral acceleration at exit is:

$$
a_{\text{peak}} = \frac{q_0 \, S \, C_L}{m} = \frac{\rho_0 \, v_0^2 \, S \, C_L}{2m}
$$

Setting this equal to a g-limit and solving for the maximum density (minimum altitude):

$$
\rho_{0,\text{max}} = \frac{2 \, m \, a_{\text{limit}}}{v_0^2 \, S \, C_L}
$$

| Payload class | $g$-limit | Min exit altitude (conservative) | Min exit altitude (purpose-built) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bulk cargo | 30 g | ~14 km | ~17 km |
| Rugged cargo | 20 g | ~16 km | ~19 km |
| Sensitive cargo | 10 g | ~21 km | ~24 km |
| Human-rated at 3 km/s (brief 5g peak) | 5–7 g | ~24–26 km | ~27–29 km |

The table applies at 3 km/s, where exit pressure at 20 km is about 401 kPa. The human vehicle uses a lower speed.

**The human vehicle exits at ~700 m/s.** At that speed, exit dynamic pressure is only ~22 kPa, gentler than a rocket's Max Q. The peak lateral g at full bank ($C_L = 0.5$) is:

$$
a_{\text{peak}} = \frac{\rho_0 \, v_0^2 \, C_L \, g}{2 \, R \, W_L} = \frac{0.089 \times 490{,}000 \times 0.5 \times 9.81}{2 \times 5.53 \times 2{,}000} \approx 1.0g
$$

The vehicle banks at full authority with g-loads at level flight. The aero delta-v at full $C_L = 0.5$:

$$
\Delta v_{\text{aero}} = \frac{\rho_0 \, v_0 \, H \, C_L \, g}{2 \, R \, W_L} = \frac{0.089 \times 700 \times 7500 \times 0.5 \times 9.81}{2 \times 5.53 \times 2000} \approx 105 \text{ m/s}
$$

Aero credit scales with speed; peak load scales with its square. At 700 m/s, full bank gives about 100 m/s credit at 1 g, versus about 500 m/s for cargo at 3 km/s. Most human-case savings come from the electromagnetic climb and reduced ascent losses.

### <span id="appendix-c3" class="appendix-anchor"></span>C3. Cargo Structural and Thermal Survivability at Exit

*The cargo reference design exits at 3 km/s, 20 km altitude, Mach ~10. Dynamic pressure is 401 kPa—roughly 10× a rocket's Max Q. The calculations below estimate the brief thermal and structural loads that follow.*

**Reference vehicle.** 50 t payload, 10% dry fraction, hydrolox propellant for 7.2 km/s engine duty. Total mass at exit $m_{\text{exit}} \approx 484$ t. Planform $S \approx 490$ m² (~31 m × 16 m) set by runway landing wing loading.

#### 1. Aerodynamic Loads

Speed of sound at 20 km: $c = \sqrt{\gamma R T} = \sqrt{1.4 \times 287 \times 216.65} \approx 295$ m/s. Mach number at exit: $M = 3000/295 \approx 10.2$.

Dynamic pressure: $q_0 = \tfrac{1}{2} \rho_0 v_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.089 \times 9 \times 10^6 = 401$ kPa.

Stagnation (total) pressure at the nose: Using the normal-shock Rayleigh Pitot formula at $M = 10.2$:

$$
\frac{P_{\text{stag}}}{P_{\text{static}}} \approx \frac{P_2}{P_1} \times \frac{P_{0_2}}{P_2} \approx 116.5 \times 1.107 \approx 129
$$

$$
P_{\text{stag}} = 129 \times 5{,}475 \text{ Pa} \approx 706 \text{ kPa} = 7.1 \text{ bar}
$$

For scale, 7.1 bar is below a fire extinguisher's 12–15 bar and a scuba cylinder's 200–300 bar. Those pressure ratings do not establish the strength of this heated lifting body; the following estimates address its loads.

**Force decomposition during the aero bank** ($C_L = 0.5$, $L/D = 2$):

- Lift (centripetal): $F_L = C_L \cdot q_0 \cdot S = 0.5 \times 401{,}000 \times 490 = 98.2$ MN → $98.2 / (484{,}000 \times 9.81) \approx 20.7\ g$
- Drag (axial deceleration): $F_D = F_L / (L/D) = 49.1$ MN → $10.3\ g$
- Combined load vector: $\sqrt{20.7^2 + 10.3^2} \approx \mathbf{23\ g}$ peak

**Duration.** The aerodynamic forces decay exponentially as the vehicle climbs into thinning air. The e-folding time is $\tau = H/v_0 = 7500/3000 = 2.5$ s.

| Time after exit | Force as % of peak | Cumulative impulse |
|---|---|---|
| 0 s | 100% | 0% |
| 1.7 s | 50% | 50% |
| 2.5 s | 37% | 63% |
| 5.0 s | 14% | 86% |
| 7.5 s | 5% | 95% |

The model stays above half peak force for 1.7 seconds and delivers 95% of its impulse within 7.5 seconds.

#### 2. Thermal Loading

Using the Sutton-Graves stagnation-point heat-flux approximation with nose radius $R_n = 1.0$ m:

$$
\dot{q}_{\text{stag}} = 1.83 \times 10^{-4} \times \left(\frac{\rho_0}{R_n}\right)^{0.5} \times v_0^3 = 1.83 \times 10^{-4} \times (0.089)^{0.5} \times (3000)^3 \approx 1.47 \text{ MW/m}^2
$$

Since density decays as $e^{-v_0 t / H}$, heating rate decays as $e^{-v_0 t / 2H}$. Integrating:

$$
Q_{\text{total}} = \int_0^\infty \dot{q}_0 \, e^{-v_0 t / 2H} \, dt = \dot{q}_0 \cdot \frac{2H}{v_0} = 1.47 \times 10^6 \times \frac{2 \times 7500}{3000} \approx \mathbf{7.4 \text{ MJ/m}^2}
$$

**Comparison to known re-entry systems:**

| Event | Peak heat flux | Duration | Total heat load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cargo exit (3 km/s, 20 km) | 1.47 MW/m² | ~5 s effective | **7.4 MJ/m²** |
| Shuttle re-entry (orbital) | ~500 kW/m² | ~1,200 s | ~600 MJ/m² |
| Dragon re-entry (orbital, PICA-X) | ~3–5 MW/m² | ~300–500 s | ~1,500 MJ/m² |
| Apollo lunar re-entry | ~50 MW/m² | ~200 s | ~10,000 MJ/m² |

The cargo exit total heat load is **~200× less than Dragon's re-entry**. Dragon's PICA-X heat shield absorbs ~1,500 MJ/m². The cargo vehicle nose needs a few millimetres of ablator (graphite or PICA) to absorb 7.4 MJ/m². The rest of the vehicle surface sees roughly 10–30% of stagnation heating, reducing the average body heat load to ~0.7–2.2 MJ/m²—manageable with standard aerothermal coatings.

#### 3. Structural Analysis

**Wing root bending** (dominant structural load). Lift of 98.2 MN acts across half the span (~8 m average moment arm). Bending moment at wing root:

$$
M_{\text{bend}} = \frac{98.2}{2} \times 4 \approx 196\ \text{MN}\cdot\text{m}
$$

For a realistic CFRP wing-root box beam (root chord 16 m, thickness ratio 7%, structural material 25% of cross-section, $c = 0.56$ m to neutral axis):

$$
A_{\text{struct}} \approx 16 \times 1.12 \times 0.25 \approx 4.5 \text{ m}^2, \quad \sigma = \frac{M_{\text{bend}}}{A_{\text{struct}} \cdot c} = \frac{196 \times 10^6}{4.5 \times 0.56} \approx \mathbf{78 \text{ MPa}}
$$

CFRP allowable in bending: **800–1,500 MPa**. Margin: **10–19×**. Even at 100 g for hardened cargo, bending stress remains below 380 MPa—still within standard CFRP allowable with margin.

**Acoustic environment.** An attached turbulent boundary layer at Mach 10 generates surface pressure fluctuations of ~0.5–1% of dynamic pressure (RMS). At $q = 401$ kPa, RMS fluctuation ~2–4 kPa → **~160–166 dB OASPL** for ~3 seconds. This matches the acoustic environment at rocket Max Q (Falcon 9: ~160 dB; Shuttle: ~162 dB) and is within the qualification envelope of military-rated avionics. Duration is 3 seconds vs a rocket's sustained Max Q. Fatigue cycles are negligible.

#### 4. Integrated Stress Comparison: Exit vs Re-entry

Compare peak loads separately from duration and integrated exposure:

| Load | Cargo exit (3 km/s, 20 km) | Dragon orbital re-entry | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peak g | ~23 g | ~4–6 g sustained (longer) |—|
| Total aero impulse | ~570 m/s equivalent | ~7,800 m/s equivalent | **~14× less** |
| Peak heat flux | 1.47 MW/m² | 3–5 MW/m² | 2–3× less |
| Total heat load (stag) | 7.4 MJ/m² | ~1,500 MJ/m² | **200× less** |
| Acoustic duration at ~160 dB | ~3 s | ~0 s (no acoustic pulse on re-entry) |—|
| Wing root bending stress | ~78 MPa | Governed by landing | CFRP margin 10× |

Exit has higher peak g than the capsule comparison, but about one-fourteenth its total impulse and one-two-hundredth its stagnation heat load. Those are different design demands.

#### 5. Design Requirements Summary

For a ruggedised cargo lifting body (≤30 g class, standard cargo):

1. **Nose TPS:** Light ablative cap, ~5–10 mm PICA or graphite. Absorbs 7.4 MJ/m² at stagnation. Off-the-shelf aerospace ablatives (AVCOAT, PICA, SLA-561) all qualify with margin.
2. **Wing structure:** CFRP box-beam root, sized by landing loads (not exit loads). Exit bending at 23 g peak produces ~78 MPa vs 800+ MPa allowable—**10× margin**.
3. **Vehicle components:** Qualified to ~160 dB, 30 g for 3 seconds. Standard military-spec avionics envelope. The cargo payload itself (bulk metal, propellant, water) requires no special treatment.
4. **Attitude control:** Must maintain bank angle ±5° through the aero pulse. At 3 km/s and high dynamic pressure, control authority from aerodynamic surfaces is large. Small deflections produce large moments. This is a flight dynamics problem with well-characterised solutions.
5. **Hardened cargo (100 g class):** At 100 g, bending stress reaches ~380 MPa—still within CFRP limits but requiring careful layup optimisation. Heat load unchanged (same altitude, same duration). Acoustic environment unchanged. These vehicles are functionally ICBM re-entry bodies in reverse: a well-understood engineering class.<br/>

The estimates point towards a short, intense load handled with aerospace structures and thermal protection. The vehicle still needs an integrated structural, aerothermal, and control design; the component comparisons set targets for that work.

*The rocket equation punishes brute force exponentially; it rewards layered optimisation just as exponentially.*

The 64% cargo propellant saving versus all-rocket kerolox has three distinct sources. They compound through the rocket equation but can be separated:

| Step | Change | Isp (s) | Δv (km/s) | Propellant (t) | Incremental saving |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline: all-rocket kerolox |—| 380 | 9.2 | 1,081 |—|
| 1. Switch to vacuum hydrolox | Isp 380→465 | 465 | 9.2 | 653 | 428 t (61% of total) |
| 2. Eliminate losses | Δv 9.2→7.7 | 465 | 7.7 | 441 | 212 t (30% of total) |
| 3. Aerodynamic turn | Δv 7.7→7.2 | 465 | 7.2 | 385 | 56 t (8% of total) |

Vacuum hydrolox supplies 61% of the total saving in this sequence; avoided losses supply 30%, and the aerodynamic turn 8%. The shares depend on the order of decomposition.

**Why the layers compound.** The rocket equation is $m_{propellant} = m_{final} \times (e^{\Delta v / v_e} - 1)$. The exponent is $\Delta v / v_e$. The Bridge attacks both sides of that fraction simultaneously:

- *Numerator* (reduce $\Delta v$): loss elimination removes ~1.5 km/s; the aerodynamic turn harvests another 100–500 m/s. Both are free, paid for by electricity and thin air.
- *Denominator* (increase $v_e$): the Isp switch from sea-level kerolox ($v_e$ = 3,728 m/s) to vacuum hydrolox ($v_e$ = 4,562 m/s) raises exhaust velocity by 22%.

The exponent falls from $9.2/3.73=2.47$ to $7.2/4.56=1.58$, a 36% reduction. Mass ratio falls from 11.8 to 4.85 (59%), while propellant falls by 64%. Changing both required velocity and exhaust velocity compounds through the same equation.

### <span id="appendix-e" class="appendix-anchor"></span>E. Launcher Acceleration and Path Length

*The mountain provides the structure; the bore provides the path.*

Required acceleration track length: $s = v^2 / (2a)$

| Exit speed | 3 g (human ref) | 5 g (crew peak) | 10 g (cargo gentle) | 20 g (cargo ref) | 30 g (cargo max) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5 km/s | 38.2 km | 22.9 km | 11.5 km | 5.7 km | 3.8 km |
| 2.0 km/s | 68.0 km | 40.8 km | 20.4 km | 10.2 km | 6.8 km |
| 2.5 km/s | 106.2 km | 63.7 km | 31.9 km | 15.9 km | 10.6 km |
| 3.0 km/s | 153.0 km | 91.8 km | 45.9 km | 23.0 km | 15.3 km |

The reference bore is ~30 km: 12 km linear + 18 km curvilinear toward vertical. Both cargo and human flights use the same bore. Crew at 3 g sustained reach ~850 m/s summit velocity (700 m/s at pylon exit after gravity losses), navigating the curvilinear section at 5–7 g centripetal. Cargo at 20+ g sustained accelerates through both sections, reaching up to 3 km/s. The shared curve produces ~30–50 g centripetal at ruggedised-cargo speeds (~2 km/s) and ~85–90 g at the full 3 km/s, within the 100 g hardened-cargo rating (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>).

The 57 km Gotthard Base Tunnel establishes a length comparison for the 30 km bore. The launcher adds demanding vacuum, alignment, and pulsed-power requirements.

**Vacuum loading.** Atmospheric pressure is about 101 kPa. At a rock density of 2,300 kg/m³, overburden adds roughly 23 kPa per metre, exceeding atmospheric pressure at five metres depth. Gotthard's 2,300 m maximum overburden imposed roughly 50 MPa and produced 0.5–0.7 m convergence in squeezing rock. Vacuum adds a small load by comparison; airtightness and movement tolerance remain separate requirements.

The launcher needs millimetre coil alignment over tens of kilometres, tolerance of thermal cycling during multi-gigawatt pulses, and vacuum-tight joints and transitions.

**Curvilinear section.** Near the summit, the tunnel curves from its bore angle toward near-vertical alignment with the pylon. In this section, the electromagnetic coils maintain velocity against gravity (~1 g of deceleration at near-vertical orientation) but do not add speed. The dominant force the payload experiences is centripetal, from the curved path. The turn radius scales as $r = v^2/a$, which makes exit velocity the dominant variable:

| Vehicle | Summit velocity | Centripetal g | Turn radius | Arc for 70° turn | Arc for 10° turn |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cargo | 3,039 m/s | 20 g | 47 km | ~57 km | ~8 km |
| Cargo | 3,039 m/s | 30 g | 31 km | ~38 km | ~5 km |
| Human | 852 m/s | 5 g | 15 km | ~18 km |—|
| Human | 852 m/s | 7 g | 10 km | ~13 km |—|

The shared bore uses a graduated clothoid. Humans spend about 21 seconds in the curve at 852 m/s, reaching 7 g for under three seconds. Cargo reaches roughly 30–50 g at ruggedised speeds and 85–90 g at 3 km/s. Coils maintain human velocity in the curve while cargo continues accelerating.

**Load distribution in the curve.** The vehicle does not sit on rails. It rides inside a cylindrical electromagnetic cradle: a 360° magnetic suspension that distributes centripetal force omnidirectionally around the hull. At 50 g, a 500-tonne cargo vehicle has an apparent weight of ~25,000 tonnes. Concentrated on a chassis, that would require massive reinforcement. Distributed uniformly around a cylindrical shell, it becomes like the hoop stress of a deep-sea vessel.

The cradle distributes the load around a CFRP shell; the tunnel lining transfers the reaction into bedrock. Shell stability under the resulting pressure is the relevant structural design problem.

**Terminal pylon.** Vehicles coast through 10–15 km of added height. Cargo near 3 km/s loses about 39 m/s in four seconds; humans entering near 852 m/s lose about 152 m/s in sixteen seconds and exit near 700 m/s. The mountain carries launch reactions. The pylon carries its own structural, alignment, and vacuum duties.

**Passenger seat orientation: phase-by-phase.** The human body's tolerance to acceleration is strongly axis-dependent. Longitudinal Gs acting head-to-toe (+G$_z$) drain blood from the brain; the onset of G-induced loss of consciousness (G-LOC) occurs around 5–6 g. Transverse Gs acting chest-to-back (+G$_x$, "eyeballs-in") keep blood at the same hydrostatic level as the heart and brain; humans remain conscious and functional up to 10–15 g in this orientation.<sup><a href="#ref-20">[20]</a></sup> Every crewed vehicle design—from Mercury to Dragon—exploits this by seating astronauts supine relative to the thrust axis, turning what would be a blackout into a heavy but survivable sensation.

The Bridge vehicle faces a complication standard rockets do not: the dominant force vector rotates through roughly 70° over the first two minutes, from longitudinal (linear bore) to centripetal (curvilinear section) and back. The solution is fully articulating seats that track the shifting vector continuously.

| Phase | Duration | Peak g | Force axis | Seat position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linear bore | ~29 s | 3 g | Longitudinal (forward) | Reclined ~15–20° from horizontal; force is +G$_x$ |
| Curvilinear section | ~21 s | 7 g (3 s) | Centripetal (radially inward) | Seats pivot to flat; centripetal load acts chest-to-back, +G$_x$ |
| Pylon coast | ~16 s | ~0 g | None | Seats return upright |
| Aerodynamic bank | ~11 s | ~1 g | Lateral (banking) | Upright; barely perceptible roll, like a commercial turn |
| Engine burn (0.9–5 g) | ~7 min | 5 g | Longitudinal (rearward) | Reclined again; seats tilt further as acceleration builds to 5 g at burnout |
| Re-entry glide | ~30 min | ~1.5 g | Longitudinal (braking) | Standard seated posture; spaceplane deceleration well within comfort envelope |

Entering the curve requires the seats to follow the local force vector. A 7 g pulse along the spine threatens loss of consciousness; reclining redirects it chest-to-back for the roughly three-second peak. The design therefore needs an articulating seat and restraint system qualified for the changing load.


### <span id="appendix-f" class="appendix-anchor"></span>F. Terminal Structure Materials

*The terminal pylon remains the weakest engineering link in the architecture.*

Carbon nanotube composites are the enabling material for the terminal pylon. The key distinction is between tensile and compressive behaviour:

| Property | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| CNT fibre tensile strength (macro-scale) | 4–7 GPa typical; up to 14 GPa demonstrated | Peng et al. (2008),<sup><a href="#ref-21">[21]</a></sup> recent macro-fibre results |
| CNT fibre compressive strength | ~416 MPa (pure), ~573 MPa (epoxy-infiltrated) | Literature survey |
| Specific tensile strength | ~46,000 kN·m/kg (theoretical individual tube) | |
| Carbon fibre (T1000G) tensile strength | 6.4 GPa | Commercial reference |
| High-strength steel tensile | 1.0–2.0 GPa | |
| Kevlar 49 tensile | 3.0 GPa | |

The order-of-magnitude disparity between CNT tensile and compressive strength drives the architecture toward a tension-stabilised stayed mast rather than a compression tower. In this design:

- The central mast carries modest compression (its own weight only: payloads coast through at exit velocity with no acceleration forces transmitted to the structure)
- A wide network of guy cables, anchored to bedrock kilometres from the base, carries the dominant structural loads in tension
- The mast can be slender because the cables prevent buckling and there are no launch reaction forces

This is the same structural principle as a guyed radio mast, which routinely reaches 600+ m with far weaker materials. Scaling to 10–20 km above a mountain summit requires centurial-grade CNT composites, but the *type* of structure is well understood.

**The unresolved structure.** Multi-gigapascal fibres and a suitable structural principle do not establish a 10–20 km aligned, vacuum-integrated terminal with adequate dynamic margins. The essay treats it as a programme of materials and construction work across decades. A lower terminal could retain part of the atmospheric benefit.

Wind loading at stratospheric altitude is far lower than at sea level. Dynamic pressure scales with density, and at 20–30 km altitude, air density is about 1.5–7.3% of surface values. The dominant concern shifts from wind to thermal cycling, alignment maintenance under differential solar heating, and vacuum seal integrity.


### <span id="appendix-f2" class="appendix-anchor"></span>F2. Phase Zero: Angled Exit Without Pylon

The pylon is the hardest component in the architecture. It is also not required for first operations.

For ruggedised bulk cargo—raw materials, propellant feedstock, structural mass, water—a launcher can exit directly from the mountainside at a steep angle (45–60° from horizontal), skipping the pylon entirely. At the extreme end, expendable probes at 50–100 g need only 5–10 km of tunnel and can tolerate the full atmospheric exit pulse. The tunnel is the only major infrastructure: bored mostly horizontal through the mountain for the acceleration phase, curving to the exit angle in the final kilometres.

**The air is denser and more useful.** At an 8 km summit exit with no pylon, air density is ~0.53 kg/m³ (43% of sea level). Dynamic pressure at 3 km/s is roughly 2,400 kPa, about 6× the 20 km reference case. The structural pulse is fierce but brief (~2.5 s time constant). For ruggedised cargo at 30 g peak tolerance, the aerodynamic turn harvests ~735 m/s of free horizontal delta-v, more than the 500 m/s available at 20 km with 20 g. At 100 g, the aero turn yields ~2,450 m/s. At that loading, though, the vehicle is closer to a ballistic shell than a lifting body. The aero delta-v formula starts to break down as the turn becomes a large fraction of total velocity.

$$
\Delta v_{\text{aero}} = \frac{a_{\text{peak}} \cdot H}{v_0} = \frac{30 \times 9.81 \times 7500}{3000} \approx 735 \text{ m/s (at 30\,g)}
$$

**The angled exit adds a horizontal head start.** At 60° from horizontal, the vehicle exits with ~1,500 m/s horizontal and ~2,600 m/s vertical. At 45°, the split is ~2,120 / 2,120. The vehicle is already partway to orbit before the bank begins. Combined with the aero turn, the engine's residual duty drops further.

**Tunnel length scales inversely with g-tolerance.** This is the expendable-probe argument. At 100 g and 3 km/s, the tunnel is ~4.6 km, a fraction of the Gotthard. At 50 g: ~9.2 km. At 30 g: ~15.3 km. For disposable shells carrying bulk mass (water, metal stock, shielding aggregate), the vehicle cost is minimal and the tunnel is short. The economics resemble artillery logistics more than aerospace: cheap rounds, expensive gun, fire often.

| Configuration | Tunnel g | Tunnel length | Exit altitude | Aero Δv | Vehicle type | Pylon |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expendable probe (100 g) | 100 g | ~4.6 km | ~8 km | ~2,450 m/s | Disposable shell | No |
| Expendable probe (50 g) | 50 g | ~9.2 km | ~8 km | ~1,225 m/s | Disposable shell | No |
| Ruggedised drone (30 g) | 30 g | ~15.3 km | ~8 km | ~735 m/s | Reusable drone-bus | No |
| Reference cargo (20+ g, 3 km/s) | 20+ g | ~30 km (shared) | 20 km | ~500 m/s | Reusable lifting body | Yes (12 km) |
| Reference human (3 g, 700 m/s) | 3 g | ~30 km (shared) | 20 km | ~105 m/s | Crewed space-plane | Yes (12 km) |

**What the pylon adds.** Moving the exit from 8 to 20 km reduces peak dynamic pressure about sixfold. That broadens the payload range to electronics, optics, biological material, and precision instruments. The pylon-free case serves ruggedised bulk: water, metal, propellant, and shielding.

**The bootstrap path.** The angled tunnel remains a bulk-freight route when a pylon is added. A vertical exit then serves sensitive cargo and people.

The pylon unlocks the full system. The tunnel alone is enough to start.

### <span id="appendix-i" class="appendix-anchor"></span>I. Environmental Comparison

**The problem with scaling chemical rocketry.** NOAA research (2022) found that a projected 10× increase in hydrocarbon-fuelled launches would cause meaningful damage to the stratospheric ozone layer, primarily through injection of black carbon (soot) and reactive nitrogen species directly into the stratosphere.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> NASA's 2024 review found that launch and reentry particulate emissions could, by 2040, become comparable to the natural meteoritic background flux if launch activity continues its growth trajectory.<sup><a href="#ref-27">[27]</a></sup>

The environmental case for the bridge rests on those effects becoming a constraint as throughput rises.

**Launcher-assisted architecture comparison.**

| Architecture | Lower-atmosphere combustion | Stratospheric black carbon | Ozone impact | Propellant waste in atmosphere |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kerolox all-rocket | Full stack | High (soot from RP-1) | Significant at scale | ~1,081 t per 100 t payload |
| Launcher + aero turn + hydrolox | Upper stage only, above ~25 km | Near zero (H₂/O₂ exhaust is water) | Low (water vapour, OH chemistry at scale) | ~385 t water vapour above ~25 km |
| Launcher + aero turn + NTP | Upper stage only, above ~25 km | Zero (no combustion) | Minimal (H₂ exhaust) | ~126 t hydrogen above ~25 km |

The launcher replaces first-stage combustion with electricity. A hydrolox orbital stage still emits high-altitude water vapour, with the following consequences.

**High-altitude water vapour injection is not zero-impact.** Three mechanisms deserve acknowledgement:

*Noctilucent cloud seeding.* Water injected above ~80 km (the mesosphere) can nucleate polar mesospheric clouds. NASA directly observed Space Shuttle exhaust creating artificial noctilucent clouds. This is primarily an optical effect, not a serious climate driver, but it is a real and observable atmospheric perturbation.

*Hydroxyl (OH) chemistry.* Water vapour in the stratosphere photodissociates under UV to produce OH radicals, which participate in catalytic ozone destruction cycles. The effect is much smaller per molecule than halogen-based chemistry (HCl from solid rocket motors is far more damaging), but at high launch rates, stratospheric OH perturbation warrants quantitative modelling.

*Residence time.* Stratospheric water vapour has a longer residence time than tropospheric, on the order of months to years rather than days. Injecting water above ~25 km means it stays aloft long enough for cumulative effects to matter at industrial throughput.

The comparison assigns black carbon roughly 500× the per-mass warming potential of CO₂ at injection altitude. D's reference propellant savings are about 64% for hydrolox and 88% for NTP. Less propellant per payload reduces injection per launch; higher cadence can still increase annual totals.

At hundreds of thousands to millions of tonnes a year, atmospheric chemistry needs measurement and modelling. N covers the additional industrial and orbital burdens.

**Mass economics.** For a reusable hydrolox bridge vehicle doing ~7.7 km/s after launcher handoff, the rocket equation gives mass ratio $m_0/m_f = e^{7700/4560} \approx 5.4$. The ratio of total launched mass to delivered payload depends critically on the vehicle's dry-mass fraction $f_d$:

|---|---|

### <span id="appendix-m" class="appendix-anchor"></span>M. Dry Fraction Sensitivity and Energy Budget Decomposition

*A heavier reusable vehicle needs more propellant, which adds weight of its own. This appendix follows that compounding cost through the energy budget.*

**Where the energy goes.** For each kilogram of net payload delivered to LEO via the launcher-assisted hydrolox architecture, the energy budget breaks down into three terms:

1. **Electric throw** (launcher kinetic energy + potential energy): $E_{throw} = \tfrac{1}{2}v^2 + gh$. At $v$ = 3 km/s, $h$ = 20 km, $\eta$ = 60–70%: ~1.9–2.2 kWh/kg launched. This is a *minority share* of total energy.

|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 2.68 | 1.50 | ~113 |
| 0.15 | 2.97 | 1.67 | ~125 |
| 0.20 | 3.39 | 1.90 | ~143 |

The NTP table uses stage-based dry fraction (structure divided by structure plus propellant); hydrolox uses gross-based dry fraction (structure divided by total launched mass). Do not compare equal-numbered rows directly. NTP reduces propellant mass, but producing hydrogen still costs energy. Its stated benefits concern mass and trip time.<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>


### <span id="appendix-n" class="appendix-anchor"></span>N. Environmental Externalities at Scale

*Hydrogen replaces soot with water vapour. Higher throughput brings new demands on land, water, and orbital traffic. This appendix follows those costs.*

**Atmospheric baseline.** <a class="xref" href="#appendix-i">Appendix I</a> compares exhaust and its chemistry. This design aims to avoid measurable regression from the cited Montreal recovery trajectory, which extends to about 2066. The ozone constraint remains alongside the new burdens below.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#ref-33">[33]</a></sup>

**What the bridge introduces or shifts.**

*Re-entry metals.* Measurements find spacecraft metals in stratospheric aerosols. Thousands of annual returns require atmospheric monitoring, materials selection, ablation management, and planned re-entry corridors.<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>

*Orbital traffic.* NASA and U.S. Government standards set disposal and debris-mitigation requirements. Hourly operations need traffic management and reliable disposal within the limited useful corridors.<sup><a href="#ref-16">[16]</a></sup>

*Land and water.* At roughly 8 Mt/yr of complexity exports, O implies hundreds of gigawatts of installed PV. Solar land, electrolysis water, and cryogenic plants all require siting, investment, and permits. <a class="xref" href="#appendix-o">Appendix O</a> <sup><a href="#ref-17">[17]</a></sup>

*Nuclear propulsion.* Earth-adjacent fission operations require launch approval, operational safeguards, and an end-state disposal plan. The IAEA/UNCOPUOS framework provides the international baseline.<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#ref-34">[34]</a></sup>

**Net assessment.** The proposal reduces soot and propellant use while adding power infrastructure and more orbital operations. Its advantage must survive the costs of land, water, re-entry aerosols, debris control, and any nuclear stage.

### <span id="appendix-p" class="appendix-anchor"></span>P. Lunar ISRU Energy Intensity

*The Moon's mass driver is energy-cheap. Processing the regolith is not.*

Lunar launch uses about 0.78 kWh/kg kinetically, or 1.12–1.31 kWh/kg at 60–70% efficiency. The more demanding task is producing metals, glass, and oxygen from regolith. See <a class="xref" href="#appendix-h">Appendix H</a>.

NASA's ISRU requirements documents note prototype oxygen extraction systems operating at approximately **400 kWh/kg O₂** (annualised, including all system overhead).<sup><a href="#ref-38">[38]</a></sup> That is two orders of magnitude larger than the launch energy itself. At 500 Mt/yr of processed export, even if only 20% of the mass requires full extraction-grade processing, the power demand reaches hundreds of GW—still a tiny fraction of the Moon's ~12.9 PW intercepted sunlight, but the dominant term in the lunar energy budget.

**Processing sets the industrial scale.** Beneficiation, thermal recuperation, reactor design, and electrolysis must lower energy intensity. NASA's GaLORE work addresses the chemistry; mature efficiency remains to be demonstrated.<sup><a href="#ref-39">[39]</a></sup>

| Lunar process | Energy intensity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mass driver launch | ~1.1 kWh/kg | Kinetic + efficiency losses; the easy part |
| Oxygen extraction (prototype) | ~400 kWh/kg O₂ | NASA ISRU annualised; target is orders of magnitude lower |
| Metal smelting (analogous) | ~15–25 kWh/kg | Terrestrial aluminium smelting analogue |
| Total processing (speculative mature) | ~30–100 kWh/kg | High uncertainty; depends on beneficiation breakthroughs |

The uncertain processing intensity, rather than the known escape energy, dominates this comparison.


### <span id="appendix-q" class="appendix-anchor"></span>Q. How the Bridge Changes the SSTO Mass Budget

*The launcher is the first stage—made of bedrock and electricity instead of aluminium and kerosene, used thousands of times, never discarded.*

*The single-stage-to-orbit spaceplane has been pursued since the 1960s (X-20 Dyna-Soar, X-33/VentureStar, Skylon). It has never closed. The mass budget explains why—and why the Bridge changes the arithmetic.*

**The SSTO mass budget.** For a reusable vehicle with dry fraction $f_d$ (structure as a fraction of gross liftoff mass), the payload fraction is:

$$
f_{payload} = \frac{1}{R} - f_d
$$

where $R = e^{\Delta v / v_e}$ is the mass ratio. If $f_{payload} \leq 0$, the vehicle cannot reach orbit with any useful cargo.

| Scenario | $\Delta v$ | $I_{sp}$ | $v_e$ | $R$ | $1/R$ | Payload at 8% dry | Payload at 10% dry | Payload at 12% dry |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ground SSTO, kerolox avg | 9.2 km/s | 330 s | 3.24 km/s | 17.1 | 5.8% | **−2.2%** | **−4.2%** | **−6.2%** |
| Ground SSTO, hydrolox avg | 9.2 km/s | 420 s | 4.12 km/s | 9.3 | 10.7% | 2.7% | 0.7% | **−1.3%** |
| **Bridge cargo, vacuum hydrolox** | **7.2 km/s** | **465 s** | **4.56 km/s** | **4.85** | **20.6%** | **12.6%** | **10.6%** | **8.6%** |
| Bridge human, vacuum hydrolox | 7.8 km/s | 465 s | 4.56 km/s | 5.53 | 18.1% | 10.1% | 8.1% | 6.1% |

**Ground kerolox.** Payload is negative throughout the table's dry-fraction range.

**Ground hydrolox.** At 8% dry fraction, the assumed trajectory-average 420 s engine leaves 2.7% payload: 2.7 t from a 100 t vehicle. A reusable crewed design needs a demanding mass allowance. X-33's composite hydrogen-tank difficulties illustrate that constraint.

**Bridge-assisted SSTO has more margin in this model.** At the assumed 10% dry fraction, cargo payload is 10.6% and human payload is 8.1%. A 485 t vehicle delivers 50 t of cargo. Structure, thermal protection, and reserves must fit within that dry-mass allowance.

**What changed.** D2 separates engine performance from reduced velocity duty. Here those changes enlarge the mass remaining for structure and payload:

1. **Velocity duty:** the cargo budget falls from 9.2 to 7.2 km/s through electromagnetic climb and aerodynamic credit.

2. **Engine performance:** the comparison rises from a 420 s trajectory average to 465 s in vacuum, an 11% increase.

3. **Vehicle design:** the stage avoids a conventional powered ascent through dense air. Its lifting body must instead survive C3's high-pressure cargo exit, perform the bank, and return to a runway within the dry-mass allowance.

From ground kerolox to bridge hydrolox, the exponent falls from $9.2/3.24=2.84$ to $7.2/4.56=1.58$ (44%). Mass ratio falls from 17.1 to 4.85 (72%). At 10% dry fraction, the latter leaves 10.6% for cargo.

**Other assists.** Air launch supplies roughly 250 m/s at 10 km—about 3% of orbital speed. The bridge proposes a larger change in starting conditions and engine duty.

Faster sea-level assists face much higher dynamic pressure. Elevation reduces that exposure while changing the bank and burn conditions. The proposed gain depends on all three stages working together.

The model creates payload margin by moving the starting conditions. Demonstrating a reusable vehicle inside that margin is the remaining design task.


### <span id="appendix-r" class="appendix-anchor"></span>R. The Falsifier Register

*What would change the case for the bridge?*

Recorded July 2026. The essay is a centurial project, so the register separates what can fail by a date, what can fail only by recomputation, and what is declared as modelling.

**R.1 — The necessity claim, by 2035.** §I rests on published modelling that ozone and climate damage from chemical launch rise non-linearly at roughly ten times today's cadence, while moving civilisation off-Earth needs hundreds of times (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-i">Appendix I</a>, <a class="xref" href="#appendix-n">Appendix N</a>). Launch cadence is public and stratospheric monitoring is continuous. If cadence grows by an order of magnitude by 2035 and measurement shows no regression against the Montreal recovery trajectory, the ceiling sits higher than modelled and the bridge's necessity argument weakens to an economics argument. The bridge would still be the railroad. The horse would be healthier than claimed.

**R.2 — The rocket floor, both directions, by 2040.** The essay puts reusable rockets at \$50–200/kg and hands them the bootstrap: they seed the lunar industry that justifies Meridian. The claim can fail both ways, so the register watches both. If sustained commercial pricing has not reached ~\$200/kg by 2040, the bootstrap stalls and the cathedral chain slips a phase. If pricing instead falls well below \$50/kg while cadence scales without R.1's limit binding, Meridian's window narrows to the bulk-throughput regime no rocket fleet reaches—the 400–1,000× of §I and <a class="xref" href="#appendix-g">Appendix G</a>—and the bridge becomes a later, larger project than the essay implies.

**R.3 — The materials gate, by 2045.** <a class="xref" href="#appendix-f">Appendix F</a> names the pylon the weakest link. Macro-scale CNT fibres run 4–7 GPa today, with 14 GPa demonstrated, and the tension-stabilised mast is a known structural type. The gap is production. If commercial-volume CNT fibre has not passed ~10 GPa by 2045, the 20 km terminal recedes a generation and the architecture falls back to what <a class="xref" href="#appendix-f2">Appendix F2</a> already prices: angled exits at 8 km serving ruggedised bulk cargo, where most early tonnage lives anyway. The timetable dies. The physics does not.

**R.4 — The arithmetic, checkable today.** Some claims wait on no event; they fail by recomputation. The atmosphere tax: 9.2 km/s from the ground against 7.8 km/s in orbit (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-b">Appendix B</a>). The 95% line: that share of atmospheric mass below 25 km (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-a">Appendix A</a>). And the Pythagorean Dividend carries two design points that must not be conflated: the crewed vehicle exits at 700 m/s and banks for ~100 m/s of horizontal gain at a 7 m/s vertical tax—one per cent—while the 3 km/s cargo shot gains 500 m/s at a 42 m/s tax—1.4 per cent (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-c2">Appendix C2</a>). The body quotes the crewed case, because that is the flight the reader takes. A reader with a spreadsheet can attack every number in this entry this afternoon. That is the point.

**R.5 — Declared modelling, no honest date.** The lunar arithmetic is modelling on published constants, not measurement: \$0.04/kg is launch energy at lunar solar prices (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-j">Appendix J</a>), and the honest dominant term is processing—prototype oxygen extraction runs near 400 kWh/kg, two orders of magnitude above launch energy (<a class="xref" href="#appendix-p">Appendix P</a>). If ISRU processing energy does not fall by at least an order of magnitude from the prototype figure, the Moon stays a laboratory, §V's asymmetry weakens, and the habitats of §VI wait on Earth exports the launcher alone cannot supply. No date is honest here. The number to watch is NASA-published ISRU energy intensity, and the register names it so the claim cannot drift into folklore.

**Declared interest, July 2026.** The author holds no commercial stake in this architecture. He intends to build an Age of Wonders infrastructure group making access bets across the sectors this canon names. The register exists so that intention can never quietly grade its own homework.
