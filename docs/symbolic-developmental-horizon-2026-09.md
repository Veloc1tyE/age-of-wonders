## The integration

The hypothesis now concerns the continuing cost of sustaining a mapmaker, including after its developed state has been copied. Experience changes attention, memory, and the physical processes that shape subsequent learning. The author wagers that preserving their generative effects over longer adaptive histories requires exponentially more digital work, over a range that makes emulation impractical while living implementation remains viable. This is an explicit substrate-efficiency and economic wager, not an assertion that digital state cannot be copied.

The candidate anomaly is biological learning's combination of transfer, continuing adaptation, and physical economy. The sources establish components, not a controlled biological–digital mapmaking cost differential. The decisive causal bridge is whether retaining discovery capacity—not one exact neural trajectory—requires progressively expensive digital fidelity. The economic conclusion requires this cost to survive compression, copying, alternative algorithms, and shared improvements. No hard takeoff additionally requires it to bind before broadly superior AI research arrives.

Weather motivates the temporal cost question. Exponential error growth makes useful forecast horizon logarithmic in starting accuracy. A compute claim needs a further cost–accuracy relationship. Appendix F now gives the essay's own model directly: at fixed operating speed and reliability, an assumed exponential power–adaptive-horizon curve implies logarithmic horizon gains from a larger power budget. Neither its exponent nor its applicable range has been measured. A slower simulation can trade power for elapsed time; competitive cost must account for both.

## Computational irreducibility: the final thread

The proposed resistance to shortcuts now has its own mechanism. Earlier encounters change what later encounters can teach. A reduction that preserves today's answers can lose the capacity for tomorrow's discovery. The author wagers that some of this adaptive work must unfold, even when its rules and starting information are available. Biological computationalism identifies a physical candidate in coupled activity, memory, and plasticity; it does not prove the candidate computationally irreducible.

The three steps remain distinct: necessary adaptive work; rising digital cost of sustaining it; a practical resource threshold that binds before hard takeoff. Irreducibility motivates the first. It neither supplies the exponential cost curve nor prevents the work running faster on other hardware. Biology's proposed advantage is economical execution, not exemption from the work.
